# Grenada — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: 0–40% under the CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET); agricultural lines average ~19.6% MFN, some bound up to 100%
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 15% standard rate; 0% on basic food staples, medicines and exports
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — only the non-reciprocal Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI), giving Grenada-origin goods duty-free entry to the US
- Seasonal import restrictions: No confirmed current seasonal bans; import licensing is automatic except for whole chicken, eggs and war toys
- Top imported food products: Not found at a granular food-category level; overall import bill is dominated by fuel, with food among other major categories
- Top exported food products: Nutmeg, mace, cocoa, fish

## Opportunities for Our Products
Grenada is a small, tourism-driven economy that imports far more than it exports — in 2024, Grenada exported $53.8M and imported $793.1M, a trade deficit of $739.3M, led on the export side by nutmeg, cocoa, fish, and electronic components . That imbalance is actually good news for us: it tells us this is fundamentally an import-dependent market for most consumer food categories outside of its traditional spice/cocoa/fish exports, and the region as a whole is actively worried about this — CARICOM's food import bill stands at roughly $13.7 billion , which is why governments are pushing import-substitution policy (something we cover more under Protected Products below, since it does affect a couple of our categories).

Poultry and eggs are the clearest example of a protected-but-still-import-reliant category for us: licensing requirements are applicable to a few agricultural goods, such as whole chicken and eggs , which signals the government is actively managing these specific product lines to shield local producers — exactly the kind of regional dynamic described where Caribbean governments and the regional poultry industry have pushed for increased protection because imports have tended to be more competitively priced than domestically produced poultry meat, with poultry subject to the CARICOM Common External Tariff . That means whole chicken and shell eggs need us to work through a licence every time, but it doesn't close the door — it just means we need a local buyer/importer relationship and the right paperwork lined up (see Import Permits below). Processed, cut, or further-prepared poultry products generally move more freely under automatic licensing.

Beef, pork, and general meat products are a solid opening for us because Grenada's own production base is thin and it already has an established import pathway — the eligible product categories for export into Grenada explicitly include beef and beef products, poultry and poultry products, and pork and pork products , with all federally inspected US meat and poultry establishments eligible to export to Grenada . On the buyer side, tourism/hospitality is almost certainly our best entry point — hotels, resorts, and dive operators are treated as their own tax category (10% VAT on hotel accommodation versus 15% standard), which reflects how central tourism is to the whole economy, and that sector is a dependable, higher-volume, higher-spec buyer for meat, seafood, dairy-adjacent, and specialty produce compared with general retail.

Fresh produce is a more mixed picture: Grenada still has a large subsistence sector producing fruits, vegetables and livestock for domestic and regional markets , so for basic tropical produce we're competing with local growers, not filling a gap. Our better produce opening is in items Grenada doesn't grow well or in volume (temperate fruit, certain vegetables, specialty/processed produce for hotel kitchens), rather than trying to compete head-on with nutmeg, cocoa, or staple tropical fruit — those are the protected, farmer-organized export commodities (see Protected Products).

Grains are largely an import-dependent category across the whole OECS, and while we didn't find a current Grenada-specific breakdown of grain import volumes, the general CARICOM pattern of heavy reliance on imported wheat, flour, and feed grains (historically significant enough that Grenada restricted some of these items under regional trade rules) suggests this is a steady, low-glamour but reliable product line for us rather than a growth story.

Seafood is the one category where we should be realistic about our positioning: fish is one of Grenada's own leading export commodities , so this is less a "sell into Grenada" opportunity for us and more a potential sourcing opportunity if we ever want to buy Grenadian fish for resale elsewhere (including into our US market, where Grenada-origin fish can move duty-free under CBI — more on that below).

## Import Rules for Food

### Tariffs & Duties
Grenada runs its import duties through the CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET), which is the primary governing structure for Grenada's import duty, establishing a harmonized trade environment, with most duties calculated on the CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight) value of goods . Headline CET rates run from 0% for essential items to 40% for luxury goods based on HS Code classification , and a regional reference source similarly puts Grenada's duty range at 0–45%, wider than the CET headline suggests . For us specifically in agriculture and food, the picture is more protective than the general CET bands imply: tariff protection on agricultural imports is high, with most agricultural lines bound at 100% under WTO rules, while the simple average of applied Most Favoured Nation tariffs for agriculture is 19.6% . On top of the tariff itself, every shipment into Grenada (and the wider OECS) picks up a Customs Service Charge of 3–6% levied on all imports in Dominica, Grenada, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines — so we should always budget duty + CSC together when quoting landed cost, not duty alone. Goods certified as CARICOM-origin move differently: goods certified as CARICOM origin enter duty-free between member states, but national consumption taxes still apply — that's not us as an outside supplier, but it matters if we're ever competing against CARICOM-sourced competitors in the same Grenadian market, since they'll have a structural duty advantage we don't.

### Taxes
Grenada's VAT (locally also referred to as GCT) sits at a standard rate of 15% , administered by the Inland Revenue Division. The good news for our core business is that basic food items, medicines, and exports are zero-rated , and more specifically 0% VAT applies to select essentials such as supplying staple food or water — this is a real cost advantage on the basic/staple end of our product list. Tourism-sector sales (hotels, dive operations) carry a reduced 10% VAT rate , which is one more reason the hospitality channel is attractive for us: it's taxed more lightly than standard retail. Businesses only need to register for VAT once annual taxable turnover exceeds EC$300,000 per year , though smaller operations can register voluntarily — relevant if we're setting up or working through a small local distributor.

### Import Permits, Licences & Documents
The backbone of Grenada's licensing system is the Supplies Control Act: the import licensing system is regulated by the Supplies Control Act CAP. 314 of the Revised Laws of Grenada and the Supplies Control Regulations, and is administered by the Ministry of Finance and Trade through its Consumer Affairs Division , with all import licences processed by the Ministry of Environment, Foreign Trade and Export Development through its Licensing Division . For the vast majority of our product lines, licensing is automatic — the exceptions that actually require a licence are whole chicken (tariff heading 0207.12), eggs (tariff heading 0407.003) and war toys , so those two product lines need extra lead time on paperwork.

Meat, poultry and eggs carry an additional layer: for meat and eggs, a Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) certificate is necessary from the Ministry of Agriculture prior to the approval of the licence , and separately the importer must obtain an import permit issued by veterinary authorities of the Grenada Ministry of Agriculture for entry of all meat and poultry product . For fresh produce and planting material, an import permit is required from the Pest Management Unit, Ministry of Agriculture to import planting materials, fruits and vegetables .

On documentation, a standard shipment needs a Commercial Invoice, Bill of Lading or Air Waybill, Packing List, CARICOM Certificate of Value and Origin, and Import Permits for restricted items , and customs is processed through the ASYCUDA World platform administered by the Customs and Excise Division . For the actual animal-product permit process, any person intending to import animals or animal products must submit a written request to the Veterinary and Livestock Division for an import permit, including country of origin, species or product type, and name/location of the farm or facility of origin , after which the Veterinary Authority issues an Official Import Permit upon verification of an Export Health Certificate from the exporting country . Worth flagging for us: no animal or animal product may be landed at the port of entry unless authorised by the Veterinary Officer or Quarantine Officer, who will require submission of both the Import Permit and the Export Health Certificate — so these documents need to be in hand before the ship/plane lands, not sorted out after.

### Labeling & Standards
The Grenada Bureau of Standards (GDBS) is our key technical gatekeeper here: the GDBS is tasked with developing and implementing national standards for food products, ensuring food products meet established quality and safety standards before reaching consumers, and conducts testing and certification of food products for compliance with local and international standards . The specific labeling standard that applies to most of our packaged goods is GDS 1: Part 4: 2018 — Specification for the Labelling of Prepackaged Foods , alongside GDS 1: Part 1: 2021 on general labelling requirements and GDS 1: Part 2: 2021 on specific requirements for pre-packaged goods . One very simple but easy-to-miss rule: the GDBS has reminded the public to read labels carefully, and all labels presented must be in English — any Spanish/French-only labeling on our packaging needs an English sticker or relabel before it can go to retail. Beyond labeling, the Customs and Excise Division also plays an enforcement role: it is responsible for monitoring the importation of food products, ensuring imported food items comply with national safety standards, and preventing entry of substandard or potentially harmful food products .

### Prohibited & Restricted Products
There isn't a long list of flatly banned food items for us, but there is active trade-policy maneuvering we should track. In 2020, Grenada abolished licensing requirements for pasta, aerated beverages, curry powder, and margarine, replacing them with higher tariffs, by applying Article 164 of the Revised Treaty of Chaguaramas to suspend CARICOM CET treatment of those products — none of those are our core lines, but it's a useful precedent showing Grenada is willing to swap licensing restrictions for tariff protection when it wants to shield a domestic producer. Historically, Grenada has also used import quotas and restrictions elsewhere in agriculture: an older regional trade-policy assessment noted Grenada continues to impose import quotas on beer and malt from regional producers, intended to boost domestic production of those goods , and that Grenada has applied Article 29 of the CARICOM Annex in respect of brewery products, and Article 56 to restrict imports of wheat, flour, feeds and some wooden products . We'd flag this as legacy/background context rather than a confirmed current rule for our purposes — worth a direct check with our Grenadian customs broker before we commit to a wheat, flour or feed-grain shipment, since we could not verify whether those specific Article 56 restrictions are still active today.

### Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
For every meat, poultry, egg, or live-animal-origin shipment, we're dealing with a formal risk-assessment and certification process. Where risk is determined to be negligible, the importer obtains an Export Health Certificate from the competent authority in the exporting country, and upon verification the Veterinary Authority issues an Official Import Permit ; where risk is judged significant, the Veterinary Authority will refuse the application and formally notify the importer of the findings and the decision to deny the import permit — so SPS risk assessment is a real gating step, not a formality, and we should build in time for it. On the food-safety side more broadly, Grenada's Food Safety Act gives the regulator (referred to in the Act as "the Authority") the power to require advance notice and inspection: the Minister may publish a list of foods that require inspection by an inspector at a port of entry before exportation from Grenada or upon importation, and an importer or exporter of any listed food must ensure the required compliance steps are followed, and any food imported into Grenada and found to present a food safety risk shall be re-exported to the exporter or to such place as the Authority may direct — a good reminder to make sure our paperwork and product condition are airtight, since a rejected shipment doesn't just get destroyed, it gets sent back to us. If we ever look at sourcing fresh produce out of Grenada (rather than just selling into it), the relevant standard is the Grenada Bureau of Standards, which determines whether fresh produce is "fit for export" — meaning it meets approved standards and is suitable for export without being prejudicial to the agricultural interest of Grenada under the Exportation of Fresh Produce Act.

## Free Trade Agreements
There is no reciprocal US–Grenada free trade agreement, and we should be clear with our own team that the program everyone refers to — the Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI) — is not one: the Caribbean Basin Initiative is not a trade agreement but a non-reciprocal grant providing special duty-free access to the US market for goods made in the CBI region . In practical terms, the CBI is intended to facilitate the development of stable Caribbean Basin economies by providing beneficiary countries with duty-free access to the US market for most goods , and it has real staying power — CBERA was implemented January 1, 1984 and has no set expiration date, while the CBTPA expansion is scheduled to expire September 30, 2030 . This matters most for the direction we'd least expect: it doesn't lower Grenada's tariffs on anything we ship from the US into Grenada, but it does mean that if we ever want to buy Grenadian agricultural products (nutmeg, cocoa, fish, processed foods) and resell them into our US market, those goods can often enter the US duty-free. That's not a hypothetical for Grenada specifically — over 90% of imports from Grenada into the US already claim CBI preferential benefits, primarily in agricultural products , which tells us Grenadian exporters and their US buyers are already using this channel heavily. On the other side of Grenada's trade relationships, it's also a full member of CARICOM and the CARICOM Single Market and Economy (CSME), and a party to the CARIFORUM-EU Economic Partnership Agreement (EPA) — useful context if we ever compete against European suppliers in the Grenadian market, since EU-origin goods get preferential treatment there that we as a US-based (non-EU, non-CARICOM) supplier do not.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
We could not find confirmed, current seasonal import bans, quota windows, or harvest-protection licensing specific to produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains or seafood. What we do know is that Grenada's general import licensing regime is overwhelmingly automatic: licensing is automatically granted except for whole chicken, eggs and war toys , which are managed year-round rather than seasonally as far as we could verify. The one documented example of a standing (not seasonal) quota is Grenada's ongoing import quota on beer and malt from regional producers, intended to boost domestic production of those goods — again, not a food category we deal in, but evidence that Grenada does use quotas when it wants to protect a specific local industry. Given the gap in current sourcing here, we'd recommend confirming directly with a Grenadian customs broker or the Ministry of Agriculture before planning any shipment timed around a local harvest window (e.g., domestic fruit/vegetable seasons), rather than assuming none exists.

## Protected / Reserved Products
This is the one area where Grenada runs genuine state-sanctioned monopolies, and it's directly relevant if we ever want to buy Grenada's two most famous export commodities rather than just sell into the market. Cocoa is the clearest case: the Grenada Cocoa Association is the sole purchaser and exporter of cocoa in Grenada, and it is prohibited for any person other than the Association to purchase cocoa in Grenada except for domestic use or under licence from the Board . In practice this means all cocoa exports have to go through the association to be sold abroad, and cocoa farmers cannot decide who to sell to — only to the Association at prices it fixes itself . Nutmeg sits under a parallel structure via the Grenada Co-operative Nutmeg Association. For us, the takeaway is simple: we cannot just contract directly with a Grenadian cocoa or nutmeg farmer and export the beans/spice ourselves — we'd need to buy through (or under licence from) the relevant Association, and pricing is administered rather than market-negotiated. There has been periodic government talk of merging and liberalizing these two boards, but as of the most recent reporting we found, efforts to merge the Grenada Co-operative Nutmeg Association and the Grenada Cocoa Association and liberalise the industry have stalled, with the team appointed to work on it yet to convene a meeting — so we shouldn't plan around liberalization happening soon.

On the flip side, poultry and eggs are "protected" from a different angle — not a state monopoly, but a licensing mechanism (whole chicken and eggs require import licences, per the Import Permits section above) that functions to shield local poultry producers from import competition, consistent with the wider regional push described where Caribbean governments and the regional poultry association have sought increased protection for the domestic poultry industry because imports have tended to be more competitively priced .

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
The trade pattern here is pretty stark and tells us a lot about where we fit. On the export side, Grenada's identity is built around a small set of traditional agricultural commodities: Grenada's 2024 exports were led by nutmeg, cocoa, fish, and electronic components , and separately Grenada is renowned for its spices and is the leading producer of cinnamon, cloves, ginger, and nutmeg . Grenada also holds a genuinely unique niche worth knowing about: Grenada is the only country authorized to export fresh soursop to the United States market — not something we'd compete with, but useful market intelligence if we ever look at diversifying what we bring back from Grenada into the US.

On the import side, the headline figure is that Grenada imported $793.1M in 2024 against only $53.8M in exports — a roughly 15-to-1 import-to-export ratio that underscores just how import-dependent this market is for nearly everything outside its traditional spice/cocoa/fish exports. We weren't able to find a current, granular commodity-level breakdown of Grenada's food imports specifically (e.g., exact volumes of imported poultry, beef, rice, or dairy), so we'd treat "top imported food products" as an open research item for our next deeper-dive rather than guess at it. What the pattern does suggest for us: Grenada is structurally a net food importer across almost every category except its own traditional exports, which lines up with our Opportunities assessment above — meat, poultry (non-whole-chicken), eggs (with licence), grains, and non-competing produce are where the real volume opportunity sits, while nutmeg, cocoa, and fish are categories where Grenada is a competitor/source, not a customer, for us.

## Other Market Context
Currency risk is unusually low for us here compared with most of our other markets: Grenada uses the East Caribbean dollar, issued within the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union, fixed at approximately EC$2.70 per US$1 , a peg that has held since 1976 and is managed by the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank (ECCB). That's genuinely useful for us when we're quoting USD-denominated contracts into Grenada, since this arrangement reduces exchange-rate uncertainty for transactions linked to the United States, though it does not remove exposure to imported inflation, shipping costs, or changes in global interest rates — in other words, our pricing risk shifts from currency swings to freight/fuel cost swings, which is a different (and more familiar) kind of risk for us to manage.

On the demand side, tourism is the structural backbone of the economy and shapes who our best buyers are: the fact that hotel accommodation and diving activities get their own reduced VAT bracket is itself a signal of how much policy weight that sector carries, and it's consistent with the broader regional picture where tourism-driven demand for meat, seafood and quality produce tends to be more consistent and higher-spec than general retail demand. Regionally, the push to shrink the CARICOM-wide food import bill (the $13.7 billion regional figure cited at the 2026 Caribbean Week of Agriculture) is worth watching, since the 2026 Caribbean Week of Agriculture built on the CARICOM 2025+ Initiative geared at reducing the region's food import bill, bringing attention to investment, export expansion, climate resilience and food-security targets — this is the kind of regional policy momentum that could eventually translate into more import-substitution pressure (like the chicken/egg licensing or the cocoa/nutmeg monopolies) on categories we currently rely on being open. Finally, on logistics: customs is processed digitally through the ASYCUDA World platform administered by the Customs and Excise Division , which is a plus for us in terms of predictable, trackable customs processing compared with fully paper-based systems.
