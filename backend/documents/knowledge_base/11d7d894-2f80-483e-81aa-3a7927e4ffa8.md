# Colombia — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: MFN simple average ~6.8% overall (2025); sensitive ag goods like beef, rice, dairy can run 20%-98%
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 19% standard rate; basic foodstuffs (canasta familiar) taxed at 0%
- Free trade agreement with the USA: Yes — CTPA in force since 2012, phasing out most remaining ag tariffs by 2030
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes — grain/dairy import licensing tied to domestic harvest "absorption" agreements, plus disease-driven bans (e.g., avian flu)
- Top imported food products: Corn, soybean meal, wheat, ethanol, pork
- Top exported food products: Coffee, bananas, cut flowers, avocados

## Import Tariffs & Duties
Colombia's tariff structure isn't one-size-fits-all, and this matters a lot depending on which product line you're moving. On the general side, Colombia's duties are mostly consolidated into three tiers: zero to 5% on capital goods, industrial goods, and raw materials not produced domestically; 10% on manufactured goods; and 15% to 20% on consumer and "sensitive" goods. The WTO pegs Colombia's simple average MFN applied tariff rate in 2025 at 6.8%, with roughly 51% of tariff lines duty-free under MFN terms .

Where it gets interesting — and where you need to pay close attention — is with the "sensitive" ag products that fall under the **Andean Price Band System (APBS)**. These fall under the Andean Price Band System established by Decision 371 of the Andean Community, which includes Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru, and protects domestic industry with a variable levy that increases tariffs when world prices fall and lowers them when world prices rise. This system covers 13 product groups across more than 150 tariff lines and can push duties above 100% depending on world commodity prices, for products including corn, wheat, rice, soybeans, pork, poultry parts, cheeses, and powdered milk. Historically that's meant real swings — one older reference showed white rice duties fluctuating between 9% and 36%, powdered milk between 17% and 36%, and yellow corn/sorghum between 15% and 40% depending on world price conditions.

For the very most protected categories outside the U.S. FTA framework, duties can be brutal: automobiles face a 35% duty; beef and rice face an 80% duty; and milk and cream face a 98% duty, while whey carries a 20% in-quota duty (on a 3,000-ton quota) but 94% outside the quota. The takeaway for you: if you're sourcing from a non-FTA country, grains/dairy/beef/rice are where you'll get hit hardest — which is exactly why leveraging the U.S.-Colombia agreement (see below) is so valuable if you're supplying from the U.S. side of your business.

## Taxes
The headline number is Colombia's VAT (called IVA), and the standard VAT rate is 19%, with a reduced rate for basic foodstuffs, health services and insurance, and a nil rate for some goods . This 19% applies on import too — it applies to imported goods and services rendered by non-residents, with the general rate applied at the moment of billing .

The good news for a food importer: many staples get relief. Colombia's VAT structure has a zero-rate tier where exports sit, along with basic food staples (the canasta familiar), prescription medicines, books, and school notebooks — and zero-rated suppliers can still reclaim their own input VAT , which is a real cash-flow advantage compared to being merely "exempt." Separately, a reduced 5% rate applies to specific items like pre-paid healthcare plans, agricultural machinery, and solar panels , so if you're also moving farm equipment alongside product, that's worth knowing.

One thing to flag if any of your business touches sugary or ultra-processed packaged foods: Colombia has been layering on a "junk food" excise tax. A 10% excise tax (15% in 2024 and 20% from 2025) is imposed on the production, import, and self-consumption of sugar- and sodium-rich ultra-processed food products, covering tariff classifications including 04.04.90.00.00, 16.01, 16.02, and 17.04. That's now at the full 20% rate, so if any of your grain/meat-adjacent processed products fall into those classifications, budget for it.

Also worth knowing if you do any small-parcel/e-commerce-style shipments: Colombia just tightened low-value import VAT rules. The VAT exclusion for imports via postal traffic, express delivery, or urgent shipments was reduced from USD 200 to USD 50 — goods exceeding USD 50 in value are now subject to import VAT. That was part of a broader emergency fiscal package — a package of emergency tax measures enacted through Legislative Decree 1474 of December 29, 2025, aimed at stabilizing public finances after Congress rejected the government's Financing Law underpinning the 2026 Budget — so keep an eye out for further ad hoc tax moves this year given Colombia's fiscal pressure.

Lastly, on beverages if that's ever adjacent to your book: liquors, wines, aperitifs and similar beverages are now subject to VAT at 19%, up from the reduced 5% rate — a sharp jump worth knowing if you ever bundle beverage lines.

## Free Trade Agreements
This is the big one for you, since the U.S. is your other core market. **The U.S.-Colombia Trade Promotion Agreement (CTPA/COTPA)** has been in force since 2012, and it's genuinely transformative for a food trader moving between these two countries. It entered into force on May 15, 2012, eliminating tariffs and other barriers to goods and services and significantly expanding trade between the two countries. On the Colombia-import side (U.S. goods going into Colombia), the agreement front-loaded a lot of value: more than half of U.S. agricultural exports became duty-free upon entry into force, with most remaining tariffs phased out over 15 years — Colombia eliminated duties on wheat, barley, soybeans, soybean meal and flour, high-quality beef, bacon, almost all fruit and vegetable products, peanuts, whey, cotton, and the vast majority of processed products. For the remaining sensitive categories, the TPA also provides duty-free access for specified volumes of standard grade beef cuts, chicken leg quarters, pork, corn, sorghum, animal feeds, rice, soybean oil, and dairy products through tariff-rate quotas (TRQs) — meaning duty-free up to a volume cap, then regular (often high) duty above it.

Timing matters here in 2026 specifically: tariffs on Colombia's most sensitive agricultural goods are scheduled to be gradually phased out between January 1, 2026, and January 1, 2030, and in the meantime U.S. agricultural exporters continue to benefit from duty-free access for certain sensitive products through tariff-rate quotas. On the U.S. Customs side, the quota mechanism is still active for 2026: goods of Colombia under HTS General Note 34, Chapter 99 subchapter 28, have a quota period running January 1 to December 31, 2026. And critically, most Colombian goods currently enter the United States free of duty and the merchandise processing fee, with virtually all entering free by the time COTPA is fully implemented in 2028. The results speak for themselves. U.S.-Colombia agricultural trade reached record highs in 2024, with $4.5 billion in U.S. exports (+21%) and $4.4 billion in Colombian exports (+8%) — corn, soybean oil, ethanol, and pork drove U.S. export growth, while coffee, flowers, bananas, and avocados fueled Colombian exports. On market share, the FTA has been decisive: despite geographic proximity to major competitors Brazil and Argentina, Colombia purchases half of its agricultural imports from the United States, with preferential access from CTPA helping the U.S. increase its market share from 19 percent prior to CTPA to 50 percent by 2018. Beyond the U.S., Colombia is not a one-trick pony on trade agreements — Colombia has 17 free trade agreements with 66 economies worldwide, including the European Union, MERCOSUR, Canada, and Mexico . That's relevant if you ever want to use Colombia as a re-export hub, but for your direct U.S.–Colombia lane, CTPA is what you build your pricing model around.

What CTPA does NOT cover: it doesn't erase the Andean Price Band System exposure for non-U.S.-origin goods, and it doesn't remove Colombia's separate sanitary/phytosanitary approval gate — a product can be tariff-free under CTPA and still get held up or blocked entirely if ICA or INVIMA doesn't sign off (more below).

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
Yes, and this is a category where a lot of new importers get tripped up because it's not really "seasonal" in the calendar sense — it's tied to protecting domestic harvest/production cycles and disease status, and it can shift with little warning.

The core mechanism is what's historically been called the **Ministry of Agriculture's "Absorption Requirement."** Ministry of Agriculture Decree 2439/94 requires the MOA to approve import licenses for commodities that compete with domestically produced goods under absorption agreements between local producers and processors, and this covers wheat, poultry meat, malting barley, corn, rice, sorghum, wheat flour, and oilseeds (soybeans, soybean meal, soybean oil). Historically this has been used quite deliberately around domestic harvest timing — for example, Colombia used its licensing system to restrict powdered milk imports specifically during the country's high milk production seasons of May-July and August-October. If you're in grains or dairy, build your shipping calendar around Colombia's harvest windows, not just your own supply availability.

The other major "restriction" category is animal-health-driven, and it's very active right now given global avian flu pressure. Colombia moves fast and defensively on this: in May 2025, ICA suspended poultry imports from Brazil after an HPAI outbreak was confirmed on a breeding poultry farm in Rio Grande do Sul, protecting Colombia's disease-free status held since 2011 — imports affected were mainly fertile eggs and day-old chicks essential to the national poultry chain. The U.S. has been hit by exactly this kind of suspension too: Colombia's Ministry of Agriculture once instructed all Colombian ports to deny import permits to any poultry products over HPAI concerns, impacting roughly $1.1 million in U.S. exports over less than a week before diplomatic pressure got it reversed. Beef has been caught up in this too — Colombia restricted beef and beef product imports from U.S. states where dairy cows tested positive for avian influenza . On the flip side, market access does get restored: on September 10, 2025, Colombia officially reopened its market to U.S. live cattle under the same import requirements as previously established .

Bottom line for you: if you're in poultry, eggs, or beef, you need a live-monitoring habit — not a one-time compliance check — because ICA can flip market access on or off within days based on disease detections anywhere in your supply chain's country of origin.

## Protected / Reserved Products
Colombia is quite open about protecting certain domestic agricultural sectors, and understanding which ones will tell you where you'll face real friction versus where the door is wide open.

The clearest protection mechanism is the **Andean Price Band System** described above — it's specifically designed to shield Colombian producers of corn, wheat, rice, soybeans, pork, poultry, cheese, and powdered milk from low world prices by ratcheting tariffs up when global prices fall. Rice is a particularly protected staple — outside of FTA quota, rice is subject to an 80 percent duty , and dairy is even more shielded, with milk and cream facing a 98 percent duty outside preferential arrangements.

Rice also illustrates that even preferential access doesn't guarantee smooth sailing — quota utilization can lag: despite CTPA advantages, U.S. rice exports to Colombia have at times declined sharply, with one report noting the Colombia rice TRQ went unfilled for the first time , reflecting how local politics and domestic absorption priorities can still constrain the practical opening even when the tariff line is technically favorable. Corn has a similar political dimension: corn production is touted in Colombia as a possible substitute crop for coca , so there's a policy motivation to protect domestic corn growers beyond pure economics — something to keep in mind if you're pitching bulk corn imports.

There's also a specific carve-out mechanism worth knowing: the corn absorption/Mercosur dynamic. Colombia typically applies duties on corn from Mercosur countries when there are downward international corn price movements, while the CTPA excludes the APBS mechanism for U.S. imports and instead uses a tariff rate quota mechanism with out-of-quota duties. That's a real structural advantage for U.S.-origin corn versus South American competitors — worth highlighting to clients sourcing feed grains.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
This is arguably the section that will affect your day-to-day operations more than tariffs will, because getting the tariff right doesn't help if your shipment gets stopped at the port for missing paperwork.

Two agencies run this show, and you need to know which one governs your product:

- **ICA (Colombian Agricultural Institute)** handles the ag/animal/plant side. Raw materials, animal products, and agricultural inputs are exempt from INVIMA registration but require a sanitary import permit from ICA, which is the authority responsible for issuing sanitary and phytosanitary import permits for products such as animal goods, fresh produce, grains, pet food, and agricultural inputs including seeds.

- **INVIMA** handles processed/human-consumption food products more broadly, alongside the Ministry of Health for pre-approval on food-for-human-consumption imports specifically. Pre-approvals for agricultural products are regulated by the Ministry of Agriculture through issuance of a sanitary or phytosanitary certificate, while for food products imported for human consumption, it's the Ministry of Health and Social Protection that provides the pre-approval. The practical mechanics: the ICA permit outlines the specific zoosanitary and SPS requirements for each shipment, importers must obtain this permit before shipment with all export documentation referencing it, and products must be inspected by the exporting country's authority prior to export and by ICA upon arrival in Colombia. In other words: get your permit lined up before you load the container, not after.

For grains specifically, rice is a good illustrative example of what "typical" SPS looks like once market access is established: there are no restrictions on paddy rice imports beyond TRQ volume limits — the only requirements are a phytosanitary certificate and pre-shipment fumigation with Phosphine or Methyl Bromide. One structural risk to flag: Colombia's food regulatory framework is a patchwork that's still catching up. Food laws and regulations are very dispersed in Colombia and in some cases haven't been updated since the 1980s, though in recent years Colombia has worked on updating nutritional regulations such as front-of-pack labeling, maximum sodium thresholds, and healthy taxes. There's also a new fortification mandate in the pipeline: in 2025 Colombia has been working on a new draft regulation for mandatory fortification of rice, wheat flour, corn flour, and processed products derived from wheat and corn flour. If you're in grains, check on the final status of this before locking in long-term supply contracts — fortification requirements can mean formulation or processing changes upstream.

Also on the customs-process side, there's a system upgrade in motion: a new Advanced Declaration requirement was not yet in force as of May 2025, since DIAN was still upgrading its IT systems, with the system expected to be operational by end of 2025 or early 2026 — once enforced, it will impact all exporters to Colombia, customs agencies, importers, and Free Trade Zone operations. Worth checking current status with your customs broker before you ship, since this changes documentation timing.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
Colombia's trade pattern tells a clear story, and it maps directly onto where you should focus your buy-side versus sell-side strategy.

**What Colombia exports (your buy-side opportunity):** Colombia is a classic tropical/high-value specialty exporter. The top ten Colombian agricultural products exported to the United States are unroasted coffee, cut flowers, bananas, roasted and instant coffee, processed fruit and vegetables, seafood products, other fresh fruits, sugar and sweeteners, avocados, and other livestock products , and together these comprise about 90 percent of Colombian agricultural exports by value to the United States. Coffee remains the anchor: Colombia ranks as the second largest supplier of unroasted coffee to the U.S. after Brazil, with 2024 exports increasing 8 percent to reach $1.4 billion. Avocados are the fastest-growing story here — Colombian avocado exports reached $91 million in 2024, a 221 percent increase — and seafood, fresh fruit, and beef/cocoa are all showing strong momentum too, per Colombia's own export data ( coffee exports grew $176 million, cocoa $17 million, and beef and offal $14.5 million in a single recent month comparison ).

**What Colombia imports (your sell-side opportunity):** This is squarely a bulk-commodity and feed/protein-input story. The top 10 U.S. exports to Colombia in 2024 were corn ($1.56 billion), soybean meal ($654 million), ethanol, pork and pork products ($360 million), distillers grains, soybeans, dairy products, other feeds/meals/fodders, wheat, and soybean oil. Corn dominates by a wide margin and has grown fast — corn imports carry a 10-year growth rate of 103% , reflecting Colombia's expanding livestock and poultry feed sector outpacing domestic grain production.

**What this means for your business:** the opportunity split is almost mirror-image. On export TO the U.S., Colombia competes on high-value, climate-driven specialty crops (coffee, tropical fruit, flowers, seafood) where U.S. domestic production simply can't match it — that's your sourcing lane if you want differentiated, premium SKUs for the U.S. market. On import INTO Colombia, the opportunity is squarely in bulk feed grains, oilseeds, and animal proteins (corn, soy, pork, dairy) where U.S. agricultural efficiency and CTPA preferential access give you a real cost and market-access edge over Mercosur competitors. Given your existing lines (produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains, seafood), you're well positioned on both sides: grains and pork/dairy inputs going into Colombia, and seafood/specialty produce potentially coming out — though note Colombia's own seafood exports are modest compared to coffee/fruit and mostly niche/processed categories, so treat that as a smaller, more curated opportunity rather than a bulk play.

## Other Market Context
A few things that will shape how smoothly (or not) your operations run in this market:

**Currency volatility is real and directional.** The Colombian peso has been on a genuinely wild ride. Forecasts earlier in 2025 projected the exchange rate at 4,350 pesos per dollar by the end of 2025 and 4,230 by December 2026 , but by mid-2026 the peso had actually strengthened well beyond that, trading around COP 3,660 per dollar in early April 2026 , with more recent reporting showing continued strength driven by remittances now outpacing combined coffee and coal export revenue, with May 2026 alone bringing in US$1.12 billion — the 24th straight month above the US$1 billion mark. But forecasters still flag medium-term depreciation pressure, with most forecasts centering on COP 4,000-4,200 by year-end and pessimistic scenarios extending to COP 4,600. Practically: if you're invoicing or getting paid in pesos, hedge or price in short cycles — the standard advice for anyone with money at stake, including small importers, is to convert in stages and not bet on hitting one exact exchange-rate level.

**Inflation and interest rates are elevated and food inflation specifically is a live pressure point.** Food inflation was running at 6.27 percent as of March 2026 , and the central bank has been holding rates high to manage this — Banco de la República's Board kept its benchmark rate unchanged at 11.25% as of its late-April/early-May 2026 decision. Some forecasts see this getting worse before better: annual inflation is projected to end 2026 at 7.0% before declining to 5.6% in 2027, prompting the central bank to raise its policy rate to 12.25%. A subsequent report puts the policy rate even higher at 12.0%, held since July . For your business, that means Colombian buyers' purchasing power for imported goods is being squeezed on the margin, even as a stronger peso partially offsets that for imports specifically.

**Political transition adds near-term uncertainty.** Colombia enters this period facing three convergent risks: a presidential election, an unresolved central bank crisis, and a suspended peace process — President Petro's term ends in August 2026. Fiscal strain is also structural, not incidental — the emergency VAT decree discussed earlier came about specifically because Congress rejected the government's Financing Law underpinning the 2026 Budget , and the central government fiscal deficit is forecast at 6.7% of GDP in 2026. Expect more ad hoc tax and customs tweaks over the coming year as the government scrambles for revenue — build flexibility into your contracts rather than assuming today's tax treatment holds for 12+ months.

**Logistics: workable but not best-in-class.** One specific pain point that matters directly to your business: the cold chain is underdeveloped and deficient in Colombia, which is an important flag if you're moving fresh meat, poultry, eggs, or seafood — plan for more conservative transit/shelf-life buffers and vet your Colombian distribution partner's cold-chain capability specifically, rather than assuming U.S.-standard infrastructure. On the positive side, port infrastructure investment is happening — Cartagena is developing as an industrial and logistics hub, with a new international airport under construction — but regulatory uncertainty, high corporate taxes, and frequent regulatory shifts remain cited as deterrents in the latest U.S. government investment climate assessment.

**Demand fundamentals are actually decent for food/beverage right now.** The local food industry has been recovering, supported by lower inflation boosting household purchasing power, with rising demand for healthy food ingredients driven by changing consumer preferences and new nutritional regulations. Retail is also shifting structurally — a slowdown in Colombia's economic growth has helped fuel the expansion of discounters, whose retail sales grew by 34 percent in 2023 to reach $7.5 billion. If you're selling into Colombia, that suggests value-tier and discounter channels are where volume growth is concentrating, not necessarily premium retail.
