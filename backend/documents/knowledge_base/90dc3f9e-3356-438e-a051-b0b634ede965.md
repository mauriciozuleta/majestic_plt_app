# Saint Lucia — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET) generally tops out at 20%, but "sensitive" agricultural lines (notably poultry) carry a 40% CET tier, with additional duties pushing some chicken products far higher
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 12.5% standard rate, but roughly 70 staple food items are zero-rated (0%)
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — one-way U.S. trade preference (CBERA/CBTPA), not a reciprocal FTA
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes, but not calendar-fixed — Saint Lucia issues case-by-case import/phytosanitary permits and can suspend categories anytime (e.g. 2020 poultry ban over avian flu)
- Top imported food products: poultry meat, other meats, rice, dairy, and general foodstuffs
- Top exported food products: bananas, mangoes, avocados, coconuts

## Import Tariffs & Duties
For us, the starting point for anything we ship into Saint Lucia is the CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET), which Saint Lucia applies as a member of the CARICOM Customs Union — Saint Lucia applies HS 2017 with 8-digit codes under the CARICOM Common External Tariff, administered by the Customs and Excise Department, Saint Lucia, within the CARICOM Customs Union, OECS, WTO framework. The general ceiling most of our non-agricultural goods will fall under is described as the CARICOM common external tariff ranges up to a maximum of 20% , and most imports except those from CARICOM nations are subject to import duties, which consist of a customs duty, a consumption tax, and a service charge. Where it gets much more relevant to our actual product lines is agriculture. CARICOM deliberately carved out a higher-protection tier for "sensitive" agricultural products, and poultry is the headline example: customs duties applied in CARICOM countries are established in their schedules at the World Trade Organization as their ceiling duties for agricultural products, and in the CARICOM Common External Tariff for sensitive agricultural products, which is 40%. On top of that base rate, member states can stack additional duties and charges, so the customs duties on imported chicken parts in individual countries can be up to 286% — a figure driven by regional food-security and rural-employment policy rather than simple protectionism, since the region needs to promote local production for food security and to provide rural employment, and there was concern about the importation of cheap chicken leg quarters from the USA, which threatened the viability of local producers. This means if leg quarters or bone-in cuts are part of our poultry business, we need country-specific confirmation of the applied rate before quoting — it will not simply be the 20% general ceiling.

More broadly, an older but still-cited regional study found that the average tariff for all goods across CARICOM is above 25 percent, while agricultural goods are subjected to a higher tariff of 40 percent , which lines up with what we're seeing in the current poultry example. We should treat 20% as our working assumption for general produce/grains and budget for up to 40% (plus possible surcharges) on livestock/meat/poultry categories until we've pulled the specific HS line.

On the flip side, there are exemption pathways that matter if we ever set up local blending, packing, or distribution infrastructure: to facilitate industrial development, the government has in specific cases negotiated the complete elimination of both import and export duties , and exemptions are granted for raw materials and plant and machinery used in manufacturing and for certain items imported by hotels under construction, extension, or refurbishing projects.

## Taxes
Our imports into Saint Lucia sit on top of the CET duty with a Value Added Tax layer administered by the Inland Revenue Department. The standard VAT rate is 12.5%. Critically for us, VAT is charged at the border on our shipments just like it is on domestic sales: taxable imports as well as local supplies of goods and services attract VAT at the same rate, and for imported products the VAT must be paid to the Customs and Excise Department together with the other customs duties and taxes at the time of importation. It's also worth flagging that the application of the CARICOM Common External Tariff in St. Lucia remains unchanged by the VAT — so duty and VAT are separate, stacking costs, not alternatives.

The good news for a lot of our core product lines is that basic foodstuffs get real relief. Going back to VAT's 2012 introduction, many basic staples such as fresh eggs, pasta, unprocessed raw chicken and fish, evaporated & powdered milk, butter, fresh potatoes, unprocessed vegetables, select fresh fruit, rice, flour, cane sugar and others were already zero-rated or exempt. And this relief has expanded recently: in 2025, Saint Lucia's legislators moved to remove Value Added Tax on up to seventy essential and everyday food items to lower food prices for Saint Lucian consumers and households , and the government clarified that some food items were exempt, and some foods were standard — they're not going to be exempt, they're going to be zero-rated, no VAT is going to be paid. Practically, this means a meaningful share of our produce, meat, poultry, egg, and grain shipments should land at 0% VAT rather than 12.5%, which is a real competitive lever on pricing — but we need to check each SKU against the current schedule since the list is specific, not a blanket "food = zero-rated" rule.

Beyond VAT, we should also be aware of excise taxes that apply to specific categories, though these mostly hit our beverage/liquor-adjacent lines rather than fresh food: excise taxes are imposed on four main categories of goods manufactured or imported into Saint Lucia, namely fuel, liquor, beer, and cigarettes.

## Free Trade Agreements
There is no reciprocal U.S.–Saint Lucia free trade agreement, which matters a lot for how we think about this market versus our U.S. side. What exists instead is a one-way U.S. trade preference program. The Caribbean Basin Initiative, a trade initiative initiated by the 1983 Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act, is a United States program that came into effect on January 1, 1984, and aimed to provide several tariff and trade benefits to many Central American and Caribbean countries. Saint Lucia is a current beneficiary: because several beneficiary countries have entered into FTAs with the U.S., there are currently 17 eligible CBERA countries, including St. Lucia. It was also upgraded under the CBTPA, and as of the most recent listing we found, as of January 1, 2018, the following countries are CBTPA beneficiaries: Barbados, Belize, Curacao, Guyana, Haiti, Jamaica, Saint Lucia, and Trinidad and Tobago. What this actually means for us: if we're exporting Saint Lucian-origin agricultural or food products (say, bananas, mangoes, or processed goods we've sourced or packed there) into the United States, many of those goods can move in duty-free, because the CBERA provides unilateral duty-free access for most goods to the U.S. market to select beneficiary countries in Central America and the Caribbean. But this is a one-way street — it does nothing to reduce the CET or VAT we pay bringing U.S. meat, poultry, grain, or seafood into Saint Lucia. For that direction, we're still fully exposed to the CARICOM tariff structure described above.

We should also flag the EU angle since it shapes who else we're competing against there: since 2007, the European Commission has had an Economic Partnership Agreement with Antigua and Barbuda, The Bahamas, Barbados, Belize, Dominica, the Dominican Republic, Grenada, Jamaica, Saint Lucia, St. Vincent and the Grenadines, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago. That EPA is gradually phasing in EU-origin goods at reduced or zero duty on a liberalization schedule, so European suppliers (dairy, processed goods, some meats) may have a cost edge on certain lines by the time full liberalization is reached — worth watching as a competitive pressure point, not an opportunity for us.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
We did not find a fixed, calendar-based seasonal import ban regime for Saint Lucia (the kind where, say, onions or carrots are blocked for a defined harvest window every year, as some neighboring islands operate). What Saint Lucia runs instead is a permit-based system that functions similarly in effect — approvals can be withheld or products can be temporarily restricted at the government's discretion, rather than being open year-round by default.

For plant products specifically, the importation and export of plant and plant material into Saint Lucia is regulated by the Plant Protection Act No. 21 of 1988, and a Plant Import Permit must be issued by the Crop Protection & Quarantine Unit of the Ministry of Agriculture before we can even attempt to ship fresh produce in. Some categories are flatly excluded regardless of season: entry status shall be determined according to the pests present in the exporting country, and importation of soft, fleshy fruits and vegetables is prohibited from all countries where the Mediterranean, Mexican, Oriental fruit flies or similar pests are established — so certain fresh fruit/vegetable sourcing regions may simply be closed to us on a standing basis, not seasonally.

On the animal-product side, we've also seen Saint Lucia use disease-triggered, temporary import bans that function like a seasonal restriction from our planning perspective. In late 2020, Saint Lucia had concerns that chicken imported from the United Kingdom and Europe may cause the spread of Avian influenza and, as a result, temporarily banned its importation from those countries , with the restriction covering fresh, chilled and frozen poultry meat from the UK and Europe with immediate effect, also applying to live birds and hatchling eggs from the UK. The USDA's own export library confirms this is an active, ongoing mechanism specifically for U.S. poultry too — FSIS maintains a running, county-by-county list of U.S. regions whose poultry is currently ineligible for export to St. Lucia due to avian flu detections, updated as outbreaks occur. For us, the practical takeaway is: don't assume a poultry or live-bird shipment is automatically clear — always check current USDA/APHIS eligibility status for the specific state/county of origin before booking, because these restrictions can appear and disappear with little notice tied to disease events rather than a predictable calendar.

Separately, on the export side (relevant if we ever source out of Saint Lucia rather than just selling in), ginger and dry coconuts require a licence from the Ministry of Agriculture to leave the country — a reminder that Saint Lucia protects a handful of its own niche export crops even in the outbound direction.

## Protected / Reserved Products
Poultry is the clearest, most consequential example of domestic-industry protection we need to plan around. The region built its highest tariff tier specifically to shield local chicken producers: in the CET Schedule (tariff heading 02.07), fresh, chilled or frozen poultry meat that is not cut in pieces is subject to the highest rate (40%) , and this exists because the continued influx of poultry meat imports has caused Caribbean governments, the Caribbean Poultry Association, and other stakeholders to push for increased protection for the regional domestic poultry industry, as imports have tended to be more competitively and lower priced than domestically produced poultry meat. If chicken (especially cut parts/leg quarters) is a product we want to move into Saint Lucia, we should expect this to be our least price-competitive category relative to local producers, given the layered 40% CET plus additional duties discussed above.

Eggs are another category where we're less likely to find an open door: the island is self-sufficient in egg production and is trying to become self-sufficient in poultry and pork production. That self-sufficiency push is backed by active government support for livestock generally — the Ministry of Agriculture is encouraging farmers to raise sheep and goats to reduce the island's importation of frozen meats, and has helped by providing support to farmers, importing bloodlines of livestock to increase the productivity of animals, and providing subsidies on animal feed. For us, this signals that meat categories where Saint Lucia is actively trying to build domestic capacity (pork, poultry, small ruminants) are the ones most likely to see policy headwinds, permit friction, or tariff protection over time — whereas categories with little domestic production (grains, most seafood species, out-of-season produce) are structurally more open to us.

Bananas also carry a layer of historical protection and government support worth knowing, even though it cuts the other way (it's an export crop for Saint Lucia, not an import-competing one for us): the banana industry is now on a decline due to strong competition from low-cost Latin American producers and reduced European trade preferences, but the government has helped revitalize the industry, with 13,734 tonnes exported in 2018. That's relevant context if we ever look at sourcing bananas or other export crops from Saint Lucia — it's a subsidized, actively-supported sector, not a purely open market.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
Every category we handle — produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains, seafood — touches Saint Lucia's SPS system in some way, so this is one of the sections we need our logistics team to internalize cold.

For anything plant-based (produce, grains, and packaging materials like wood pallets or straw), all commercial consignments of fresh fruits, vegetables and other plant products must undergo inspection certification by a Plant Quarantine officer in order to obtain a phytosanitary certificate which is needed for entry at its final port of entry , and more generally plant material such as live plants, cut flowers, craft items made of wood or straw, fruits and vegetables, seeds, and grains may harbor foreign pests not yet established in Saint Lucia and may cost the island thousands of dollars in damages to its fragile agricultural sector. Before we ship, we're expected to contact the Department of Agriculture's Plant Quarantine Unit to determine if the commodity we intend to import is an approved one, and if the commodity is allowed, submit a Plant Material Import Application form for approval. This is a real lead-time item — we should build permit lead time into our shipping schedules rather than treating it as a same-week formality, especially for any produce line we haven't shipped there before, since entry status shall be determined according to the pests present in the exporting country and a first-time commodity may trigger a full pest risk assessment.

For meat, poultry, and animal products, the requirements run through both sides of the trade — U.S. export certification and Saint Lucia's import acceptance. On the U.S. side, if we're the exporter, our supplying plants need proper FSIS certification: for fresh/frozen poultry and poultry products, except as restricted in the "Ineligible Product" section , and for beef, beef products, and beef offals produced on or after February 17, 2015, an AMS Export Verification program is no longer required , simplifying some of the paperwork versus older rules. But we absolutely need to check current regional eligibility before booking any poultry shipment, since APHIS tracks live, updating restrictions: restrictions apply to all fresh or frozen poultry and poultry products derived from birds raised, slaughtered, processed, or stored in specific affected areas during indicated dates , and products from a currently-restricted U.S. county are simply not eligible for export to Saint Lucia during that window.

For live animals and animal products generally, the import of live animals, including pets, is subject to certain restrictions, and requires an import permit from the Veterinary Service of Saint Lucia — the same office we'd deal with for any live-animal component of our supply chain.

Finally, general customs documentation applies across all our categories: all goods entering Saint Lucia must be accompanied by the necessary documentation to facilitate the customs clearance process, typically including invoices, packing lists, bills of lading, and certificates of origin.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
Saint Lucia's trade pattern tells us a lot about where we should be pushing versus where we should expect resistance. On the import side, the island is heavily import-dependent across the board: Saint Lucia is heavily import-dependent, with petroleum, food products, machinery, vehicles, and manufactured goods as primary imports , and more specifically in the food space, import goods include crude petroleum, refined petroleum, cars, poultry meats, and natural gas — poultry meat is explicitly called out as one of the country's headline imports, which tracks with everything we found on tariff protection (high protection usually signals a category with strong, persistent import demand that local production can't fully satisfy). The IMF's most recent assessment backs this up directly: St. Lucia exports tourism services and relies heavily on imported goods, including food. On the export side, Saint Lucia's food/agricultural exports are narrow and crop-specific rather than broad-based: exports include bananas, beverages, petroleum products, cardboard boxes, and agricultural products , and more specifically, crops such as bananas, mangos, and avocados continue to be grown for export. Agriculture's share of the overall economy has shrunk dramatically — agriculture, which was once the biggest industry, now contributes to less than 3% of GDP, but still accounts for 20% of jobs — which tells us local food production capacity is thin relative to population and tourist demand, reinforcing the import-dependency story rather than suggesting Saint Lucia will become a major supply source for us beyond a handful of tropical fruit/produce lines.

Put together, this points to a fairly clear opportunity map for us: Saint Lucia is overwhelmingly a market to sell into, not a sourcing market, for the core categories we deal in (meat, poultry, eggs, grains, seafood). The competitive pressure we'll face is concentrated in a few protected categories (poultry, eggs, and to a lesser extent pork), where local producers have both tariff backing and active government support. Outside those categories — general grains, most seafood, and much of the produce and meat range not raised locally — the market looks structurally open, with import dependency as the dominant fact on the ground.

## Other Market Context
A few structural points shape how we'd actually operate there day to day. Currency risk is essentially a non-issue: the Eastern Caribbean dollar has been pegged to the United States dollar at a rate of XCD 2.70 to $1.00 since 1976, and as a result, the Eastern Caribbean dollar does not fluctuate, creating a stable currency environment for trade and investment in Saint Lucia. That's genuinely useful for us when quoting multi-month contracts, since we don't need to hedge FX on the Saint Lucia side the way we might elsewhere. Repatriation is also straightforward: companies registered in Saint Lucia have the right to repatriate all capital, royalties, dividends, and profits, and there are no restrictions on the repatriation of dividends for totally foreign-owned firms , and more broadly as a member of the OECS, there are no exchange controls in Saint Lucia, and parties can invoice foreign trade transactions in any currency — meaning we can invoice in USD if that's simpler for our books.

On the demand side, the economy is tourism-driven far more than agriculture-driven, which matters because tourism volume is really what's pulling in our kind of imported food volume (hotels, resorts, cruise provisioning). Like its ECCU peers, St. Lucia predominantly imports goods and exports tourism-driven services, and St. Lucia's trade as a share of GDP is above 100 percent of GDP, significantly higher than those of Latin America and Caribbean, emerging market and developing economies, and OECD countries. The IMF's latest Article IV work shows growth cooling somewhat but expected to firm back up: the economy is expected to rebound in 2026 from a tourism pickup, and inflation was negative in 2024 from lower international food and energy prices, projected to increase to 0.8 percent in 2025. Fiscally, the government has been running primary surpluses for a few years, which is a reasonably healthy backdrop for demand: real GDP expanded by 4.7 percent in 2024, driven by robust tourism flows from the U.S. and construction activity, and fiscal performance has strengthened, supported by three consecutive years of primary surpluses. That said, there are longer-term structural constraints we should keep in mind when sizing this market: long-standing challenges remain with income per capita diverging from the US in the past decades, weak productivity, high public debt stock, and the ever-present risk of natural disasters. Finally, on the policy-shift front, the most consumer-relevant recent move is the food VAT relief discussed earlier — the government explicitly frames this as a cost-of-living measure while acknowledging its limits: the Prime Minister was careful to add that while the government has no control over the import cost of goods, it can influence the final retail price through tax relief. That's a useful line for us to remember in negotiations — Saint Lucian buyers and regulators are actively focused on landed food costs right now, and pricing that highlights our ability to work within the zero-rated categories could be a genuine selling point.
