# Barbados — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: 0-20% general CARICOM tariff, but 35% average for agricultural products with some fruit/vegetable/plant lines up to 216%
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 17.5% standard rate; most basic food items are zero-rated
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — instead we get one-way US trade preferences under CBERA/CBTPA, not a reciprocal FTA
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes — licensing/quota system on sugar, bread, fresh fruit & vegetables, pulses, red meat, poultry and eggs tied to local harvest availability
- Top imported food products: dairy, beef & beef products, poultry, eggs, corn, soybeans, fresh fruit, baked goods, non-alcoholic beverages
- Top exported food products: rum & spirits, bread/biscuits/cakes/pastries, margarine, sugar, misc. food preparations

## Import Tariffs & Duties
The big number we need to plan around is that Barbados runs on the CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET), and food is one of the categories where that tariff bites hardest. In general, Barbados has implemented CARICOM's Common External Tariff for goods, with import duties ranging from 0 – 20 percent , but some items carry a higher import duty rate such as fruits and vegetables 40 percent according to older U.S. government trade guidance. More recent (2022/2023) WTO trade policy review data confirms this is still the reality for us: tariffs range from zero to 216% levied on some fruit, vegetables, and plants, and the average MFN tariff is 35.0% for agricultural products (WTO definition) and 12.8% for non-agricultural products . That's a huge spread for our category — fresh produce specifically is one of Barbados's most protected import lines, so we need to model duty into our landed cost for any fresh fruit/veg shipment far more conservatively than for packaged or processed goods.

On top of the base duty, there's also a special "buy local" tariff kicker: a select group of items that are produced within Barbados and CARICOM (including some agricultural products) are subject to a duty rate of 60% when imported from outside the region . This matters a lot for us — it means anything Barbados or a CARICOM neighbor already grows competitively (certain vegetables, poultry, eggs) could face a 60% duty wall if we're shipping it in from outside the region, while the exact same product coming from another CARICOM country pays little or nothing. This is a strong argument for us to look at whether any part of our sourcing network could route through a CARICOM origin for these specific lines.

There's also a general environmental levy layered on top of duty: an additional 1 percent environmental levy is imposed, and all goods imported in containers that are not made of plastics, glass, metal or paperboards incur a 0.75 percent environmental levy on the cost, insurance, and freight (CIF) value . Good news buried in the 2026 budget: the new customs duty and VAT reforms will benefit importers of essential goods, such as food, personal care products, and construction materials, by introducing capped container values and shifting to a free-on-board (FOB) valuation basis for the purpose of calculating customs duty on food and personal care items and construction materials until 31 March [2027]. That's directly relevant to us — moving the duty base from CIF to FOB and capping container values should meaningfully lower our effective duty and VAT bill on food shipments into Barbados, at least through that window.

## Taxes
The standard rate we'll see on most goods and services is VAT at 17.5% on the value of a wide range of goods and services imported or supplied in Barbados by VAT-registered persons (source confirmed current as of January 2026). The much better news for our core product lines: certain supplies are zero-rated, including exports, basic food items, prescription drugs, crude oil, and the supply of certain items to the international financial services sector . In practice this means a meaningful share of what we'd actually be shipping — basic produce, meat, eggs, grains — should land at 0% VAT rather than 17.5%, which is a real competitive advantage for us versus non-food imports.

We should also flag the VAT registration threshold: the threshold for VAT registration is 200,000 Barbados dollars (BBD), but voluntary registration is permitted for persons whose annual turnover is less than BBD 200,000 — worth watching since there's a 2026 budget proposal to raise that threshold further. Separately, if we ever touch sweetened beverages or any of the four excise categories, note that excise tax is charged on four categories of goods — alcoholic beverages, tobacco products, motor vehicles, and petroleum products — at rates ranging from 5 to 60 percent, and a 10 percent excise tax before VAT is also levied on sweetened beverages . As covered above, the 2026 budget's shift to FOB valuation and capped container values for food imports is the most consequential tax development for us right now, effectively shrinking the base VAT and duty are calculated on.

## Free Trade Agreements
Here's the honest answer for our planning: there is no reciprocal U.S.–Barbados free trade agreement. What we have instead is a one-way U.S. preference program. There are currently 17 eligible CBERA countries including Barbados, and the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act provides unilateral duty-free access for most goods to the U.S. market to select beneficiary countries in Central America and the Caribbean. Barbados is also one of the more favored beneficiaries — as of January 1, 2018, Barbados is a CBTPA beneficiary , which gives it expanded duty free access to the U.S. market beyond the base CBERA list.

What this actually means for us: this program governs Barbadian exports going into the U.S., not the reverse. It doesn't lower any Barbadian import duty on the food we'd be shipping from the U.S. or Latin America into Barbados — CARICOM's CET still applies to us on the inbound side regardless of CBERA/CBTPA. Where it matters is on our sell-side, if Fresh24 (or a Barbados-based processor we work with) ever exports something like prepared/processed foods back toward the U.S. — that's the direction CBERA/CBTPA duty-free treatment would actually help us. Also worth noting: CBTPA is not permanent — the CBTPA is scheduled to expire on September 30, 2030 , so this isn't a program we should assume is a fixture forever if we build a long-term Barbados export strategy around it.

Separately, Barbados also has an EU relationship ( an Economic Partnership Agreement with the European Union has been in force since 2008, under which Barbados committed to gradual reductions in its tariffs ) and CARICOM-level bilateral deals ( through CARICOM, Barbados has reciprocal bilateral trade agreements with Colombia, Costa Rica, Cuba, and the Dominican Republic ) — none of which give us, as a U.S.-based supplier, any tariff advantage, but they matter for understanding who else is competing for shelf space in Barbados.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
This is one of the most operationally important sections for us, because it's not just theoretical — it's an active licensing regime covering several of our core categories. Permits are required for sugar, bread, fresh fruits and fresh vegetables, pulses, red meat, poultry, and eggs, and wool and mohair , and critically, the system applies to goods originating in and coming from all countries with no exception — so being a U.S. supplier doesn't exempt us. The stated purpose, straight from Barbados's own WTO filing, is exactly what we'd expect: the system is intended to regulate the quantity of imports of some agricultural products to encourage domestic production of agricultural products depending on the availability of local produce .

Poultry is the sharpest example of this in practice. The allocation of quotas in relation to poultry products is administered by the Ministry of Agricultural and Food Security, the Barbados Agriculture Development and Marketing Corporation (BADMC) is the only entity permitted to import these products, and a quarterly total of 420,000 kg is permitted for importation. In plain terms: if we want to sell poultry into Barbados, we're not selling to a general importer — we're selling into (or around) a state-controlled quota administered by a single government entity. That's a fundamentally different sales motion than a normal wholesale relationship, and it caps total market volume regardless of how competitive our pricing is.

We should also expect ad hoc, disease-driven bans on short notice — these aren't seasonal in the calendar sense but function the same way for supply planning. Barbados imposed a temporary restriction on the importation of all live birds, hatching eggs, and on fresh, frozen and chilled poultry meat and products, including table eggs originating from the UK and EU following confirmed HPAI H5N8 outbreaks in a past instance — the mechanism (Veterinary Services placing an immediate country-specific restriction after a disease outbreak) is standing policy, so any future avian flu or similar outbreak in our own supplier countries could trigger the same kind of sudden freeze on our poultry and egg shipments.

## Protected / Reserved Products
The products Barbados actively shields for domestic producers line up closely with our own core categories, which we need to take seriously in market entry planning. Beyond the licensing list above (sugar, bread, fresh fruit/veg, pulses, red meat, poultry, eggs), the tariff schedule itself is doing protectionist work: the 60% duty rate applied to select items produced within Barbados and CARICOM when imported from outside the region functions as an import-substitution wall for exactly the kind of fresh produce and possibly meat/poultry lines we'd want to sell.

Government policy is also actively pushing to shrink our addressable market over time, not just protect it statically. Barbados's annual food import bill is estimated at USD $325 million (~90% of consumption) , and the government has made cutting that bill a stated priority — CARICOM-wide, the goal has been to cut the regional food import bill by 25 per cent, with the target pushed back to 2027 . Barbados's own domestic push is concrete and current: the Onion Escalation Project is a collaborative effort with BADMC rolling out an aggressive strategic crop escalation production plan to counter climate change impacts on local farming, with 16 strategic crops targeted under the national plan . For us, onions specifically are instructive as a case study — Barbados still imports two million kilogrammes of onions annually against roughly 483,000 kg produced domestically , so there's real near-term opportunity there, but the government is explicitly trying to close that gap over the next few years, meaning today's opportunity in onions (and by extension other "strategic crops") may shrink as local production scales up.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
For our core animal-origin categories — meat, poultry, eggs, dairy — the process is permit-based on both ends of the shipment. Imports of meat and poultry, dairy, eggs, plant products, animal products, and live animals require the importer to obtain an import permit from the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security, and these products also require a sanitary export certificate from the country of origin. In other words, we (or our Barbados-side buyer) need the import permit in place before the shipment moves, and we need our own USDA/exporting-country certification lined up in parallel — these aren't sequential formalities, they need to run together in our shipment planning.

For eggs specifically, the U.S. side certification is detailed: shell eggs must be graded and certified as U.S. Grade A or better, as outlined in the United States Standards, Grades, and Weight Classes for Shell Eggs , plus flock health affidavits from an accredited veterinarian. On fresh produce, plants and plant products are separately controlled: all fruit, vegetables, seeds, plants and plant products will need to be declared at the customs department and accompanied by a Plant Quarantine Officer .

The good news for us on the general food side is that Barbados isn't a heavy pre-market-approval market for most of what we'd ship. Barbados is fairly receptive to imports of U.S. agricultural products — neither sanitary product registration, laboratory testing, special certification, nor pre-market approval is required to import the vast majority of foodstuffs into Barbados. The real friction point is labeling, not lab testing: very detailed labeling requirements for practically all food products and import licensing for some products represent the main hurdles for U.S. suppliers . This tells us where to actually invest compliance effort — get our packaging/labeling right against the Barbados National Standards (particularly BNS 5 for prepackaged meat, poultry and fish products), rather than worrying about lab certification for most non-restricted lines.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
Barbados is structurally a heavy food importer and a light, niche food exporter — and that asymmetry is exactly where our opportunity sits. As in most Caribbean islands, domestic agricultural output and food processing is limited, and Barbados relies on imports of practically the full range of food products to meet the needs of its population. On the U.S. supply side specifically, the most recent USDA data shows our likely competitive set clearly: in 2024, the top 10 U.S. exports to Barbados included Fresh Fruit ($11.59M), Dairy Products ($8.47M), Non-Alcoholic Beverages ($7.71M), Baked Goods ($7.37M), Beef & Beef Products ($7.29M), Food Preparations ($6.91M), Soybeans ($6.41M), Eggs & Products ($6.11M), Corn ($5.72M), and Poultry Meat & Products ($4.7M) . That's a direct match to our product lines (fresh produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains) and confirms Barbados already runs on U.S.-sourced supply for most of these — we're not creating a new category, we're competing for share in an established one. It's also worth noting Barbados ranks #63 among U.S. agricultural export markets with $125.66 million in total 2024 export value — small in absolute terms, but real and growing.

On the export side, Barbados's food exports are almost entirely value-added/processed rather than raw agricultural commodities — think alcohol including spirits and liqueurs (10.7% of exports), bread, biscuits, cakes and pastries (4%), margarine (2.6%), and miscellaneous food preparations (1.5%) , plus legacy sugar exports of roughly $35 million USD annually . That tells us Barbados doesn't compete with us as a raw-produce or protein supplier — it's a finished-goods exporter (rum, baked goods, condiments) — so on our sell side into Barbados there's minimal direct domestic competition in fresh produce, meat, poultry, eggs, and grains; the real competitive pressure is other established importers/distributors already serving that ~$325M food import market, not Barbadian growers.

## Other Market Context
Currency-wise, this is about as stable as Caribbean markets get for us: the Barbadian dollar has been pegged to the U.S. dollar at a rate of BBD 2.00 to USD 1.00 since 1975, and as a result the Barbados dollar does not fluctuate and can be converted to any world currency, creating a stable currency environment for trade and investment . That removes FX risk from our pricing decisions in a way most of our other markets can't offer. Repatriation is generally workable but not unconditional: companies can freely repatriate profits and capital from foreign direct investment if they are registered with the CBB at the time of investment, though the CBB has the right to stagger these conversions depending on the level of international reserves available — worth keeping in mind if we ever set up a Barbados-registered entity rather than just exporting to a local importer. All foreign currency transactions run through Authorised Dealers, who alone may legally hold and deal in foreign currencies , so we'd work through licensed local banks, not open-market FX.

Economically, the demand backdrop is decent and improving: the Barbados economy stood at an estimated $7.09 billion in 2024, making it the largest economy in the Eastern Caribbean , and the IMF forecast the Barbadian economy to grow by 3 percent by the end of 2025 , with the country ending its seven-year IMF-backed BERT program in 2025 . That said, Barbados, like most of the Eastern Caribbean, continues to grapple with supply chain delays and volatile food and fuel prices — something we should factor into freight and pricing contingency planning. On tourism, which is directly relevant to our business plan's cruise/hospitality demand thesis, the food-import dynamic is tourism-driven: hotels and cruise calls are a major structural reason Barbados needs to keep importing at scale, reinforcing why this market fits our institutional-buyer model well.
