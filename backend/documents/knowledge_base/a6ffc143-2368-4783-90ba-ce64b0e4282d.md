# United States — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: MFN base ~5% for HTS Chapters 1–24, but effective rates range 0–30%+ depending on country of origin and product
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: No federal VAT — state sales tax only, and most states exempt grocery staples
- Free trade agreement with the USA: Yes — USMCA (Mexico/Canada) plus ~20 bilateral FTAs, though 2025–2026 tariff actions have added new surcharges on top
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes — no blanket bans, but TRQ "quota year" windows and standing antidumping suspension agreements (notably Mexican tomatoes) function like seasonal protections
- Top imported food products: Coffee, seafood, fresh fruits and vegetables, beer/wine
- Top exported food products: Soybeans, corn, beef and poultry, dairy products, tree nuts

## Import Tariffs & Duties

Here's the honest picture right now: the U.S. tariff landscape has shifted dramatically over the past 18 months, and it's genuinely one of the most volatile compliance environments you'll deal with in any market. If you last checked rates a year ago, throw those numbers out.

At the broadest level, current tariffs will raise the applied tariff rate to 11.8 percent, up from 1.5 percent in 2022 , and separately, the average effective tariff rate was about 9.9% in January 2026 . For food and agriculture specifically, HTS Chapters 01-24 carry a base MFN rate of 5%, with US import tariffs on food and agriculture ranging from about 0% to 30% depending on country of origin , and highly variable rates with some products subject to tariff-rate quotas.

Layered on top of that MFN base is a patchwork of "extra" tariffs depending on where you're sourcing from:

- **Non-FTA countries generally**: Section 122 expired July 24, 2026 and was replaced the same day by the Section 301 forced-labor tariff , meaning tariffs at either 10% or 12.5% apply to 60 economies and effectively replace the global import surcharge . This stacks on top of the standard HTS duty for most commodities.
- **EU/Italy/France/Spain (olive oil, wine, cheese, pasta)**: EU sources moved to the EU-US deal's 15% all-inclusive ceiling on July 1, 2026 — French wine pays a flat 15% total, while over-quota EU dairy with base rates of 25% or more pays MFN only . Watch out for pasta specifically — 13 of Italy's biggest pasta exporters may also face an additional 91.74% anti-dumping duty on top of the existing 15%, for a combined tariff of around 107% , according to reporting cited by Yahoo Finance.
- **Brazil (coffee)**: Americans have been paying more for a cup of coffee after the U.S. imposed a 50% tariff on Brazil, the world's largest producer, in July [2025] — though note the food exemptions below have since carved out relief.
- **Mexico/Canada (USMCA)**: USMCA-compliant food products from Mexico and Canada avoid both the forced-labor surcharge and MFN duty entirely, preserving the widest price advantage . Steel/aluminum-related packaging is a notable exception — steel and aluminum from both countries face a 50% tariff regardless of USMCA status.
- **China**: effective rate is approximately 31.6% combining MFN + Section 301 + Section 122 (as of early 2026 reporting), and retaliation matters too — China announced a 15% tariff on imports of chicken, wheat, corn and cotton from the U.S. and 10% tariffs on other agricultural products , which directly affects your export-side pricing if you sell U.S. grain or poultry into China.

Importantly, there was a meaningful walk-back on food specifically: on November 14, 2025, prompted by concerns over higher food prices, the White House issued a new executive order eliminating the supplemental duties on a wide range of agricultural products, notably including beef, coffee, tea, and bananas . This tells you something useful about the policy direction — the administration is sensitive to grocery-price political blowback, so exemptions for staple/non-domestically-producible foods keep getting carved out even as broader tariff pressure rises. Still, tariffs still apply to more than half of imported food products and will continue to raise prices for consumers .

**Practical bottom line for you**: if you're sourcing meat, produce, or seafood from outside North America, price in a 10–15%+ surcharge as your baseline planning assumption, verify the current exemption list for your specific HTS codes before quoting a buyer, and treat this as a fast-moving target that needs a recheck before every large contract, not just annually.

## Taxes

Good news here relative to most markets you deal with: unlike VAT systems used in the UK, EU, and India — where a single national rate applies to most goods — the United States has no federal sales tax . There's no VAT to register for or reclaim at the border.

Instead, each of the 50 states has the authority to set its own sales tax rate, and many states allow counties and cities to add their own local rates on top . For your core product categories, this is even more favorable than it sounds: 41 states plus Washington, D.C. do not impose state-level sales tax on groceries , and most states follow Streamlined Sales Tax definitions where grocery staples — unprepared fruits, vegetables, meats, grains, and dairy — are exempt .

A handful of states still tax groceries, so if you're distributing directly to retail in these states it's worth knowing: states that still tax groceries include Mississippi (5%) and Alabama (3%) , while South Dakota taxes groceries fully, and Tennessee taxes them at a reduced 4% state rate , with Utah taxing groceries at a reduced 1.75% state rate, totaling around 3% combined with local add-ons . The important nuance for a wholesale supplier: this is a retail-point-of-sale tax, not an import duty — it typically doesn't hit you directly as an importer/wholesaler, but it matters if you're advising downstream retail customers on end pricing.

## Free Trade Agreements

The USMCA (US-Mexico-Canada Agreement) is by far the most important framework for a food/ag trader dealing with the U.S., because all agricultural products that had zero-tariff treatment under NAFTA continue to have zero tariffs under USMCA , and this has held up as a safe harbor even amid the broader 2025-26 tariff turmoil. Beyond Mexico and Canada, the US has Free Trade Agreements with 20 countries including South Korea (KORUS), Australia (AUSFTA), and several others , and goods that qualify under these agreements pay 0% or reduced tariff rates .

**What USMCA actually covers for you**: Canada provides new and expanded access via Tariff Rate Quotas for U.S. dairy exports , and on the U.S. import side, the USMCA also provides new market access in the U.S. for Canadian dairy products, peanuts, processed peanut products, sugar, and sugar-containing products through Canadian-specific tariff rate quotas, while poultry producers gained expanded access for chickens, eggs, and turkeys in Canada .

**What it takes to actually claim it**: this is where a lot of smaller traders trip up. Canadian food exports generally qualify easily if wholly grown or raised in Canada — beef, maple syrup, canola oil, pulses, and seafood all qualify and enter duty-free . But processed or repackaged goods are a trap: processed foods using non-North American ingredients may not qualify — for example, a Canadian company that packages Thai shrimp in Canada does NOT qualify for USMCA, since repackaging alone does not constitute sufficient transformation . If your seafood is caught elsewhere and merely repacked in Mexico or Canada, don't assume it's tariff-free — food and agricultural goods (HTS Ch. 1-24) generally require wholly-obtained-in-territory treatment, and sugar, dairy, and poultry have separate TRQs .

Certification itself is simpler than it used to be: the USMCA no longer requires a specific certificate of origin , and it allows the exporter, producer, or importer to self-certify origin . But be aware CBP is watching closely — CBP has significantly increased scrutiny of USMCA origin claims since 2022, driven by concerns about Chinese-origin goods routed through Mexico, and has deployed data analytics to identify suspicious import patterns . Recent data show heavy uptake: as of February 2026, approximately 86.3% of imports from Canada and Mexico were claiming USMCA preference .

**What it doesn't cover**: USMCA doesn't shield you from Section 232 steel/aluminum tariffs, doesn't eliminate sugar/dairy/peanut quotas (it just gives you a bigger slice of them), and doesn't help at all if your goods originate outside North America and merely transit through a USMCA country.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans

The U.S. doesn't operate broad seasonal import bans the way some markets protect a domestic harvest window outright, but there are two mechanisms that function similarly and matter a great deal if you trade in produce:

**The Mexican tomato suspension agreement** is the single most important one to know if you're in produce. Mexican fresh tomatoes operate under a long-running suspension agreement that holds antidumping duty at 0% in exchange for minimum export prices set quarterly by Commerce, renewed in 2024 and running through 2029 . This exists specifically because Florida growers periodically re-litigate the issue — it's essentially a permanent, quasi-seasonal protection mechanism for Florida's winter tomato season, enforced via price floors rather than a calendar ban.

**Tariff-rate quota (TRQ) "opening windows."** Sugar and dairy TRQs reset on fixed dates each year, and if you're importing under quota, timing your entry matters. USDA announced the FY2026 (October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026) sugar TRQ at 1,117,195 metric tons raw value, the WTO-committed minimum , and quota periods for products like Panama-agreement dairy run January 1 – December 31, 2026, with entries filed before 8:30 a.m. ET on opening day all receiving the same assigned time — if filings exceed the quota, accepted entries are prorated . In practice: if you're importing quota-eligible sugar or dairy, being first in line at quota opening can be the difference between duty-free and steep over-quota tariffs.

**Ongoing produce-sector investigations to watch**: avocados, berries, and most other produce currently have no active AD/CVD orders, but active investigations exist on Mexican honey and certain canned mushrooms , and Florida growers periodically push for new tomato action. For high-volume importers, it's worth monitoring the Federal Register weekly for new AD/CVD petitions that could hit your category .

## Protected / Reserved Products

A handful of commodities get structural, long-standing domestic protection that a foreign supplier needs to plan around rather than fight:

**Sugar** is the clearest case. The U.S. sugar program uses domestic marketing allotments, tariff-rate quotas, and high out-of-quota tariffs to restrict the amount of sugar available to the U.S. market, supporting U.S. sugar prices which are usually well above comparable world market prices . The basic in-quota tariff is just 0.663 cents per pound for raw sugar and 1.660 cents per pound for refined sugar — but any quantity in excess of the TRQ amount can still be imported, but at a much higher rate of duty . If you're not allocated a country quota share, over-quota sugar becomes essentially uncompetitive.

**Dairy** works similarly, with historical and lottery-based import licensing categories limiting who gets in-quota access — a legacy system that's been adjusted over the years but still functions as a gate on Y volume, not just price.

**Seafood — this is directly relevant to your business.** Several major shrimp-supplying countries currently carry meaningful AD/CVD duties: Ecuadorian shrimp companies face a 13.47 percent duty on shrimp exports to the U.S., while Indonesia has been hit with a 6.3 percent duty (2026 preliminary figures), on top of the original orders where Commerce issued an antidumping duty order on shrimp from Indonesia and countervailing duty orders on shrimp from Ecuador, India, and Vietnam back in December 2024. Older AD orders remain live too: AD orders on frozen and canned warmwater shrimp from China, India, Thailand and Vietnam, published in 2005, remain in place today . Individual company rates vary widely — a recent antidumping review hit Indian shrimp with new duties ranging from 3.76 percent to 5.08 percent, though duties against India have hit as high as 110 percent in the past — so if shrimp is part of your book, you need exporter-specific rate lookups, not just a country-level assumption.

**Other legacy trade-remedy orders worth knowing about** if you touch these commodities: garlic from China and certain honey and mushroom products have had standing antidumping orders for years, reflecting recurring pressure from U.S. growers/processors in those niches.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements

This is where the U.S. really tests new suppliers — the regulatory bar is high, but predictable once you understand the two-agency split.

**FDA governs most food (produce, seafood, packaged goods, most everything except meat/poultry/eggs).** The core mechanism is the Foreign Supplier Verification Program (FSVP): FSMA requires persons who import food into the United States to perform risk-based foreign supplier verification activities to verify the food is produced in compliance with hazard analysis and risk-based preventive controls, or produce safety standards for raw agricultural commodities . Practically, the FSVP Rule mandates importers assure all imported foods meet the same stringent food safety standards required for food products produced in the U.S. , and critically, the importer of record — the U.S. owner or consignee, or a U.S. agent if there's no U.S. owner — carries this legal responsibility . Seafood and juice have their own overlapping HACCP-based rules layered on top of general FSVP obligations.

**USDA/FSIS governs meat, poultry, and egg products separately** — and the bar here is a full country-level systems audit, not just a documentation check. Equivalence is the process by which FSIS determines whether a country's food safety inspection system achieves an appropriate level of public health protection equivalent to FSIS domestically — an equivalence determination is a prerequisite to importing these products at all . This isn't a one-time hurdle either: FSIS periodically conducts verification audits of every eligible country's inspection system to confirm it continues to maintain equivalence , and only about 33 countries are currently eligible to import meat and poultry products to the U.S. Beyond the country-level clearance, individual foreign plants need certification too: a certified establishment is one that the country's central competent authority certifies as meeting FSIS-equivalent requirements , and every shipment still faces a comprehensive reinspection system involving document reviews, on-site audits, and port-of-entry examinations of every shipment .

**What this means practically for your sourcing decisions**: if a supplier's home country isn't already on the FSIS eligible-country list for the specific meat/poultry/egg category you need, you're looking at a multi-year government-to-government process, not something you can resolve at the shipment level. For FDA-regulated goods (produce, seafood, grains, packaged foods), the burden sits more squarely on you as importer to document and verify supplier compliance — meaning your due diligence paperwork and supplier audits are your main defense against a refused entry.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products

The trade pattern here tells a clear story about where the U.S. is strong versus where it depends on imports — and it maps directly onto where you should focus energy as a trader.

**On the export side, the U.S. is a bulk commodity and protein powerhouse.** The top 10 U.S. agricultural exports in 2025 were corn, soybeans, tree nuts, dairy products, beef and beef products, vegetables, fruits, poultry meat and products, pork and pork products, and wheat . Total U.S. agricultural exports were valued at $171 billion in 2025 , and Mexico was the largest export market at 18% of the total, closely followed by Canada at $28.2 billion . This is where you'd position yourself as a distribution/export partner for U.S. grain, protein, or dairy heading into your other markets.

**On the import side, the U.S. leans on other countries for exactly the things its climate and growing seasons can't cover.** The country relies heavily on products like coffee, seafood, fresh fruits, and vegetables to meet consumer demand , and total agricultural imports were valued at $212 billion in 2025 — meaning the U.S. now runs a food trade deficit, importing more than it exports in dollar terms. Mexico and Canada are the two largest suppliers of agricultural products to the United States, providing $43.8 billion and $39.3 billion respectively in 2025 , largely counter-seasonal produce and processed goods that fill U.S. winter/off-season gaps.

**What this means for your positioning**: the opportunity for a wholesale importer is squarely in counter-seasonal fresh produce, coffee, and seafood — categories the U.S. structurally cannot self-supply year-round, especially from USMCA partners where you get the tariff advantage. The competitive pressure runs the other way for grains, proteins, and dairy — U.S. domestic production is so large and subsidized/protected (see Sugar/Dairy above) that competing on price as an importer in those categories is tough unless you're filling a specific quota allocation or a niche/organic/specialty gap.

## Other Market Context

**Currency and repatriation**: The U.S. dollar remains fully convertible with no capital controls or repatriation restrictions — a major operational advantage compared to many emerging markets. There's no requirement to route trade payments through a central bank, and USD invoicing is the global default, which simplifies your treasury planning considerably.

**Policy volatility is the defining feature right now.** As the tariff sections above show, 2025–2026 has seen an unusually rapid sequence of legal and policy shifts — IEEPA tariffs, a Supreme Court reversal, Section 122 global surcharges, and now Section 301 forced-labor tariffs, each with different rates and different exemption lists. A parallel excess industrial capacity investigation remains ongoing and likely will result in additional tariffs , so treat any rate you quote a client today as provisional and build re-verification into your contract terms (e.g., tariff pass-through clauses).

**Consumer cost pressure is real and politically sensitive**, which is actually useful intelligence for you: the current tariff structure costs the average US household approximately $1,900–$2,000 per year according to Tax Policy Center estimates, and Yale Budget Lab found tariffs' pass-through impacts on consumer prices were 46–86 percent for imported core goods . This political sensitivity is exactly why food-specific exemptions (beef, coffee, tea, bananas, etc.) keep appearing — it's a strong signal that further carve-outs for staple foods are more likely than further tariff increases on them, at least in the near term.

**Logistics**: no unusual infrastructure bottlenecks specific to food/ag beyond normal port congestion cycles; established cold-chain infrastructure exists at all major coastal and border-crossing entry points (Los Angeles/Long Beach, Laredo, Miami for perishables, etc.), which is a genuine advantage versus less-developed markets.
