# Turks and Caicos Islands — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts

- Average food-import tariff: 0% on basic foodstuffs, 10-15% on most food products, up to ~40% on specialty items (e.g. certain pelagic fish); general merchandise duty is 30-35%
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: None — no VAT or general sales tax exists in TCI
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — no reciprocal FTA; TCI has only requested (not been granted) Caribbean Basin Initiative/CBERA beneficiary status
- Seasonal import restrictions: No formal seasonal restriction on our inbound shipments, but TCI enforces seasonal export bans on conch and lobster that affect local seafood sourcing
- Top imported food products: meat & meat offal, dairy products, beverages, cereal/bakery preparations
- Top exported food products: queen conch (meat and shell), Caribbean spiny lobster

## Opportunities for Our Products

This is fundamentally a market that cannot feed itself, which is exactly the kind of market we want to be selling into. Farming employs fewer than 100 people in the entire territory, only 15 of them full-time, largely because there's so little arable land across the islands. That means virtually everything we'd classify as fresh produce, meat, poultry, eggs, and grains has to come from somewhere else — and right now, that somewhere else is overwhelmingly us (or our competitors) in the United States.

The numbers back this up clearly. The U.S. share of food and beverage exports is highest in markets such as the Turks and Caicos Islands, where the U.S. share accounts for 84 percent of that trade — and in an earlier reporting period it was even higher, with the U.S. accounting for 86 percent of the Turks and Caicos food and beverage market. That's a market where American suppliers already dominate — good news for us as a US-connected exporter, but also a reminder that competition from other US wholesalers will be fierce; this isn't a market we'd be "discovering," it's one we'd be fighting for share in.

On the import side generally, the United States remained by far the largest supplier of goods, accounting for more than $200 million in imports during the quarter, reflecting the territory's long-standing dependence on U.S. markets for food, fuel, machinery, building materials, and consumer products. And the bulletin shows strong activity in categories such as food and live animals, mineral fuels, manufactured goods, machinery and transport equipment, and chemicals, all of which are essential to supporting the tourism industry, infrastructure development, and everyday life in the islands. Food and live animals sitting right alongside fuel and machinery as a top-line import category tells us this is a steady, structural demand, not a niche one.

Looking at commodity-level data, meat and edible meat offal and dairy produce, birds' eggs, natural honey, and edible products of animal origin are consistently among the largest import groups by value, alongside preparations of cereals, flour, starch or milk, and bakers' wares . For us, that lines up well with our meat, poultry, egg, and grain-based product lines.

Who's actually buying? Given the territory's economy, the real openings are:
- **Tourism/hotels and resorts** — this is the dominant driver of food demand on the islands. High-end resort kitchens need consistent, quality-graded meat, poultry, seafood, and produce, and they're the buyers most able to absorb higher landed costs from duties and freight.
- **Retail/grocery** — supermarkets serving both the resident population and self-catering visitors are a steady channel for packaged and fresh grocery items, though more price-sensitive than hotel buyers.
- **Food service/restaurants** — a large, fragmented buyer base tied closely to tourist volume and cruise ship arrivals.
- **Food processing** is essentially not a sector here — there's no meaningful domestic processing industry to sell raw agricultural inputs into, so our opportunity is almost entirely in finished or ready-to-prepare products, not ingredients for local manufacturing.

One category where the flow could run the other way for us: seafood. If we wanted to diversify our own seafood book, TCI-origin spiny lobster and queen conch are genuine export products from the islands (more on the constraints around that below).

## Import Rules for Food

### Tariffs & Duties

The headline rate most goods face coming into TCI is steep: most items imported into the Turks and Caicos have a 30% duty, with an additional 5% Customs Processing Fee (CPF) , though other sources describe the combined effective burden as running closer to 35% on general merchandise. The good news for us specifically is that food is treated as a separate, much lighter category: food generally has a 0% duty, and building materials are generally 10% — though both of these items still incur the 5% Customs Processing Fee. Looking more specifically at food categories relevant to what we ship, some food products do not incur customs duty, most food products have duty rates of 10-15%, and some specialty foods (such as certain pelagic fish) may incur duty of around 40%. A few things for us to flag: soft drinks incur a 30% duty rate, whereas drinking water (including mineral water) incurs a 40% duty rate (35% and 45% duty inclusive of CPF) — a reminder that TCI's tariff schedule has plenty of line-item exceptions, so we should always check the specific HS code for whatever we're shipping rather than assuming the general "food" rate applies.

Importantly for how we think about this regionally: TCI is **not** inside the CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET) zone. It is only an associate member of CARICOM, not a full member subject to the CSME customs union, so there's no CARICOM-wide 0-20% banded tariff schedule to rely on here the way there would be in, say, Jamaica or Trinidad — TCI runs its own national tariff schedule instead. That said, the territory is actively pursuing full CARICOM membership, with the Acting Premier confirming in March 2026 that TCI is applying for full membership, while explicitly declining to sign onto the free movement of labour portion of the treaty. That's a trend worth watching, since full membership could eventually shift the tariff and regulatory landscape we're dealing with.

### Taxes

There's genuinely no VAT or general sales tax to plan around here. Turks and Caicos does not impose a general sales tax or value-added tax (VAT) on goods and services. There is no income tax, capital gains tax, property tax, inheritance tax, or corporation tax in the Turks and Caicos Islands; indirect taxation is primarily the duty on most imported items, and there is no general sales tax or VAT apart from a 12% tax on tourism services. That 12% tax applies to hotels, restaurants and similar tourism services — not to our import transaction directly, but it's a cost sitting on top of the supply chain once our product reaches a hotel or restaurant buyer, which is useful context when we're pricing for that channel.

For us as the importer, the practical "tax" to budget for isn't VAT — it's the Customs Processing Fee layered onto the duty. Sources differ slightly on the exact rate: one TCI-focused business resource states it's an additional 5% Customs Processing Fee (CPF) , while relocation/logistics guides cite a 7.5% Customs Processing Fee applying to the total declared value of the shipment, non-waivable , and this fee applies even if your specific item qualifies for a 0% duty rate. Given the discrepancy, we should confirm the exact current CPF rate directly with our TCI customs broker before quoting landed costs, but either way, we need to build it into every shipment's math since it applies regardless of whether the underlying duty is 0%.

### Import Permits, Licences & Documents

This is the section that matters most operationally for us, because almost everything we ship touches it. The Department of Agriculture is responsible for the management, security and oversight of all agriculturally based and supporting structures in the Turks and Caicos , and it requires import permits for four categories that map directly onto our product lines: Meat & Meat related Products; Milk & Milk related Products; Regulated Articles such as Fruits and Vegetables, Live Plants and Planting Materials, Cut Flowers, Mulch, Compost, & Bagged Soil; and Live Animals such as Dogs and Cats, Poultry, Livestock, Horses, & Other species. More specifically for meat, poultry and dairy: Turks and Caicos Islands require that importers in TCI obtain an import permit for the importation of meat, poultry and dairy products, issued in advance by the Animal Health Services Division of the TCI Department of Agriculture. This permit is secured by our buyer/importer on the TCI side, not by us directly, but we need to work closely with them so it's in place before we ship — U.S. exporters are advised to work closely with their importers in TCI to ensure that import permits for their shipments are issued in advance. The permit regime was formalized under TCI's own animal and plant health laws: Controlled Commodities under the Animal Health Ordinance 2012 include live animals such as cats and dogs or any species of animals; animal products and by-products such as pork, beef, poultry, eggs, milk and dairy products, cheese, fish and fish products , while Regulated Articles under the Plant Protection Ordinance 2012 include live plants, seedlings, vegetative stems, seeds for propagation, soil, cut flowers, fruits, vegetables, germplasm, as well as used agricultural implements or machinery. In other words, eggs and fish/seafood products are explicitly captured too — this isn't just a meat/poultry rule, it covers essentially our entire catalogue except possibly bulk dry grains.

The application itself needs specific shipment details: proposed date of entry, port of entry, country of origin and any country of transit, name of the species or item to be imported and the quantity, and means of transport. On the customs clearance side (separate from the Agriculture permit), entry is filed on a C13 Home Consumption Entry through the Click2Clear/ECAS system , and standard shipping documents apply: invoice, packing list, and bill of lading with correct HS codes for duty classification.

### Labeling & Standards SPS measures in the Turks and Caicos are regulated by the Department of Agriculture — it's the same body handling both permits and standards enforcement, so there isn't a separate dedicated food-standards agency distinct from Agriculture that we found in current sources. For labeling specifically, U.S.-origin meat needs to carry proper country-of-origin marking: products from the United States should have the mandated USDA label. Beyond that specific requirement, we did not find a detailed, TCI-specific food labeling code (e.g., nutrition panel format rules) in current sources — if this matters for a particular product line, we should confirm directly with our TCI importer/broker, since the territory in practice leans heavily on the exporting country's own labeling standards (US/USDA, in our case) rather than maintaining a distinct domestic labeling regime.

### Prohibited & Restricted Products

Several specific products need extra clearance or are flagged as restricted: mangoes (i.e., from fruit-fly free areas or hot water treated), passion fruit (pulp only), fresh guavas, fresh cherries, specific breeds of dogs, birds & fishes, meat products from countries with notifiable animal diseases, and plants from areas where specific plant diseases or pests are present all face added conditions. For commercial shipments, informal personal-import rules don't apply to us, but it's worth knowing the baseline the government applies to travelers for context: homegrown or home-processed meat products, meat products not commercially packaged, grains and produce not commercially packaged or sourced, and homegrown or home-processed grains and produce are restricted — underscoring that properly packaged, certified commercial product (which is what we ship) is the path that actually works here.

On the export side out of TCI (relevant if we ever want to buy TCI seafood), queen conch is tightly controlled: under the Fisheries Protection Ordinance, Regulation 17A, no person shall export conch during the period commencing on the fifteenth day of July and extending to and including the fifteenth day of October in each year, and this regulation includes all conch products, including meat, shells and pearls. Queen conch is an internationally threatened species protected under CITES, and in order to comply with CITES rules, TCI must enforce a closed season for export on conch products.

### Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements

For meat and animal product shipments, a sanitary export certificate needs to travel with the goods: the importation of meat and meat products requires an import permit issued by the Department of Agriculture, and a sanitary export permit from the Competent Sanitary Authority must accompany consignments. For live animals (relevant if we're ever involved in live poultry or breeding stock), an International Health Certificate must be endorsed by the Competent Authority of the exporting country before entry into TCI, and the sanitary conditions vary based on the rabies status of the country of export. US exporters of meat and poultry should also check USDA's own export library, since export requirements for meat and poultry products to TCI can be found on the USDA/FSIS Export Library.

## Free Trade Agreements

This is an area where we need to manage expectations: TCI does **not** have a reciprocal free trade agreement with the United States, and it is **not** currently a confirmed beneficiary of the Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI/CBERA) despite having asked to be one. Additional countries that are potentially eligible for CBERA benefits include Anguilla, the Cayman Islands, Sint Maarten, Suriname, and the Turks and Caicos Islands — the Turks and Caicos Islands and Sint Maarten requested CBERA status in 2012. That request has sat pending for well over a decade without resolution, which tells us not to assume any preferential US tariff treatment is coming for goods originating in TCI (not that this affects us much on the import side anyway, since we're shipping into TCI, not from it, to the US).

What TCI does have are two non-reciprocal preferences, neither involving the US: currently, the Turks and Caicos benefits from two non-reciprocal trade agreements — the Caribbean-Canada Trade Agreement, under which the Turks and Caicos enjoys a preferential tariff of 0% on exported goods to Canada, except those mentioned above , and the UK-Overseas Territories Trading Arrangement. The Turks and Caicos Islands is a member of CARIBCAN, whose objective is to promote trade, investment and provide industrial cooperation through preferential access of goods from the Commonwealth Caribbean to the Canadian market, so certain goods exported from TCI are eligible for duty-free access to Canada. What this actually means for us: these agreements govern goods going **out of** TCI to Canada or the UK — they don't lower our cost of shipping food **into** TCI from the US. If we ever use TCI as a hub to re-export product (e.g., processed or repackaged goods) toward Canada or the UK, these preferences could matter; for straightforward US-to-TCI food sales, they're not relevant to our landed cost.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans

We did not find any seasonal restriction, quota, or licensing window that specifically limits **importing** fresh produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains, or seafood into TCI to protect a domestic growing or harvest season — which makes sense given how limited local agricultural production actually is. What does exist is seasonal control on **exports** of TCI's own wild-caught seafood, which matters if we ever want to source from the islands rather than just sell into them:

- **Conch:** the Ministry of Tourism and Fisheries Department closes the conch export season from 15 July to 15 October each year, prohibiting all exports of queen conch meat for personal or commercial use.

- **Lobster:** lobster season closes for a period coinciding with the breeding season for the top fisheries export of the Turks and Caicos, allowing the spiny lobster population to be replenished before the season re-opens , and uniquely, even storage of lobster is illegal during the closed season, with fines of $5,000 or six months in prison, or both, for any breach. The open season generally runs from mid-August to April , though exact dates shift slightly year to year.

For our purposes: if we want to buy TCI-origin conch or lobster to add to our own seafood line, we need to plan around these windows and build in the fact that lobster can't even be held in inventory locally during closure — no stockpiling workaround exists.

## Protected / Reserved Products

TCI doesn't appear to run an import-substitution policy or subsidy regime protecting a domestic farming sector from our competition — there largely isn't one to protect. Farming usually generates a large percentage of jobs elsewhere, but due to the lack of arable land across all of the islands, fewer than 100 people work in agriculture in TCI, only 15 of them full-time. That's good news for us on the produce, meat, poultry, egg, and grain side: there's essentially no entrenched local industry with the political weight to push for tariff walls against us in those categories.

The one sector that genuinely functions as a protected domestic resource is the wild fishery — conch and lobster. The protections here aren't tariff-based against foreign sellers; they're conservation-driven controls (closed seasons, gear restrictions, size minimums, export bans) aimed at TCI's own fishers and processors. Efforts such as seasonal closures, gear restrictions like banning compressed air, and export quotas correlate with periods of stock stabilization , and notably, the Monterey Bay Aquarium's Seafood Watch classifies TCI conch as an "Avoid," citing depleted stocks and ineffective management. This is worth flagging internally: if we ever consider sourcing conch from TCI for resale, we'd want to factor in both the regulatory restrictions and the sustainability-rating reputational angle before committing to that as a product line.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products

The trade pattern here is about as lopsided as it gets, and it tells a clear story about where our opportunity sits. On the import side, in 2024, Turks and Caicos Islands had Product Imports of $574M , while exports were just $26.2M, making it the number 208 exporter in the world. That's roughly a 22-to-1 import-to-export ratio — TCI is structurally an import economy, and food is a core part of what it has to bring in. Within that import mix, meat and edible meat offal , dairy produce, birds' eggs, and natural honey , and cereal, flour, starch, and bakers' wares preparations are consistently top-tier import categories, and the USA supplied about 75% of total TCI imports in the data available, a dominance that's held or grown since.

On the export side, what little TCI exports is almost entirely fishery-based: major exports are lobster, dried and fresh conch, and conch shells, with the US and UK as top export partners. That's consistent with more recent trade-flow data showing the United States as the top destination for TCI food product exports. What this means for us: the competitive pressure we'd face is almost entirely on the **import side**, competing against other US (and some UK/Bahamas) suppliers already serving this market well — not from local TCI producers, who barely exist in the fresh food space. Our clearest opening is simply being a reliable, cost-competitive, compliance-ready supplier of the categories TCI already depends on imports for (meat, poultry, dairy, eggs, grains, produce), rather than trying to break into some protected niche. If we want exposure to the export side of this relationship, seafood (conch/lobster) is really the only meaningful category, and it comes with the seasonal and sustainability caveats noted above.

## Other Market Context

A few things worth keeping in mind as we think about doing business here:

**Currency and repatriation:** TCI uses the United States Dollar as its official currency, which is a real operational advantage for us — no exchange-rate risk, no currency conversion friction, and pricing/invoicing can stay in the same currency we already use domestically.

**Government revenue dependency on duties:** It's worth understanding why TCI's duty rates are what they are and why they're unlikely to drop for our benefit any time soon: customs duties (tariffs) are the primary source of revenue for the government, at 30% of total revenue, including direct duties and the customs processing fee . Because the government depends so heavily on import duty revenue in a jurisdiction with no income or corporate tax, there's limited appetite to meaningfully reduce tariffs — we should plan for these rates being a durable feature of doing business here, not a temporary friction point.

**Economic conditions:** TCI's economy was valued at roughly $1.75B GDP in 2024 , built heavily around tourism. Recent trade data shows consistent, growing import volumes: the Turks and Caicos Islands Statistics Authority recorded approximately $227 million in imports during Q4 2025 alone , and the figures reflect continued demand driven by tourism, construction, and consumer spending — a healthy, growing demand base for us to sell into, assuming tourism levels hold up.

**Logistics:** Providenciales functions as the main commercial gateway, and given how dominant US-origin goods are in TCI's import mix, shipping lanes and freight-forwarding relationships via South Florida (Miami) ports are the practical route most of our competitors already use — worth building our logistics plan around that same corridor rather than trying to establish a novel shipping route.

**Regulatory trajectory to watch:** TCI's pursuit of full CARICOM membership (noted above) is a genuine medium-term development. If it eventually joins the CARICOM Single Market and Economy as a full member, that could bring TCI under the CET and potentially change how both regional and extra-regional (i.e., our US-origin) goods are tariffed — something we should monitor rather than assume will stay static.
