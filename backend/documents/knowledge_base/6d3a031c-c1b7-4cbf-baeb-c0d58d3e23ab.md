# Sint Maarten (Dutch part) — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: 0% (general import duty has been NAf/XCG 0 since the island became a duty-free port in 1939)
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: No VAT; a 5% Business Turnover Tax (BBO/TOT) applies to local sales of goods, not to the import itself
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — Sint Maarten opted out of U.S. CBI/CBERA beneficiary status, but it already runs a unilateral 0% duty regime for everyone
- Seasonal import restrictions: Not found — no evidence of harvest-season bans or quotas, consistent with the island's negligible domestic agriculture
- Top imported food products: General "food products" as a top-level import category, with meat and poultry singled out in official import/export paperwork, mostly sourced from the US
- Top exported food products: Not found — food exports are negligible since almost all food on the island is imported

## Opportunities for Our Products
This is genuinely one of the friendliest markets we could ask for on the supply side. The island has no significant agriculture and only limited local fishing, so almost all food has to be imported, and energy resources and manufactured goods come from abroad too, primarily from Mexico and the US. That's the headline for us: there's essentially no domestic producer base we're competing against for produce, meat, poultry, eggs, or grains — we're not trying to displace a local farmer, we're filling a structural gap.

The demand side is squarely tourism-driven, which matters a lot for how we pitch ourselves. Hotels, restaurants, and related services account for about 45% of GDP, tourism brings in 73% of foreign exchange, and around one-third of the workforce is employed in tourism, with roughly 80% of visitors arriving as cruise tourists through Philipsburg. That tells us our best buyers are hotels, resorts, restaurants, and the provisioning companies that supply cruise ships and yachts — high-volume, consistent, quality-sensitive customers rather than small retail accounts. Retail/supermarket chains matter too, but the tourism food-service channel is where the real margin and volume sits for us.

The economy is also still expanding, which is good news for sustained demand rather than a one-off opportunity. St. Maarten's economy recorded solid real GDP growth of 3.4% in 2025, as stronger tourism activity and lower inflation provided support to economic expansion. Sint Maarten's economy is expected to expand by 3.1% in 2026, easing to 2.5% in 2027. Growth of that kind, driven by cruise and stay-over arrivals plus new hospitality investment, translates fairly directly into food-service demand — more covers served means more protein, produce, and grain moving through hotel and restaurant kitchens.

One thing to watch: the government itself has flagged import dependence as a vulnerability, not just an opportunity for us. Sint Maarten's economic outlook remains vulnerable to external shocks and global uncertainties, with escalating trade tensions, geopolitical instability, and higher energy prices able to disrupt supply chains, raise import costs, and fuel inflation. Policy discussion is already pointing toward diversifying trade through stronger ties with Latin America and CARICOM, alongside promoting local agriculture and reduced import dependence as a resilience strategy — worth monitoring, since any serious local-agriculture push (even a modest one) could eventually nibble at a slice of our produce and egg business, though seafood, meat, poultry, and grains are far less exposed given the island's limited land and water resources.

## Import Rules for Food

### Tariffs & Duties
The best news for us is also the simplest: there basically is no tariff. The territory has been a duty-free port since 1939, so general merchandise does not pay import duty, though every commercial shipment still has to be declared and released by Customs. Sint Maarten levies almost no import duty, and Customs Sint Maarten describes itself as mainly a law enforcement agency rather than an import duty collecting organization, with the only tax it actually levies being tax/excise on unleaded gasoline. There's no CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET) in play here either — Sint Maarten is not a CARICOM member state, so the CET framework that governs tariff treatment for CARICOM-bloc countries simply doesn't apply to us. For practical purposes, goods from inside or outside any particular trade bloc are treated the same way at the Sint Maarten border: duty-free.

### Taxes
There's no VAT or import-stage sales tax to budget for either. There is no VAT in Sint Maarten; instead, a Turnover Tax (TOT) applies to supplies of goods and services, generally at 5%. The turnover tax (BBO) is levied on the delivery of goods and all services rendered "within the territory" by resident or non-resident entrepreneurs within the scope of their business, at a rate of 5%. The catch for us, if we ever set up a local distribution arm rather than just selling to a single importer, is that this tax cascades. Because there is no input credit mechanism, the tax compounds along the chain — an importer selling to a distributor selling to a retailer produces 5% at each stage, each time on a base that already contains the previous stage's tax. So if we're selling direct to a hotel or supermarket as a single importer, our exposure is just the one layer of 5% on our sale; if goods pass through multiple local hands before reaching the end buyer, the effective burden stacks up. Worth factoring into how we price depending on who sits between us and the final kitchen or shelf.

### Import Permits, Licences & Documents
For ordinary commercial food shipments — produce, grains, packaged goods — no special import permits or licenses are required under the standard regime. Meat, poultry, and fish are the categories where documentation does kick in. Meat, poultry meat and poultry meat products shall be accompanied upon import into the territory by a Health Certificate issued by the competent health authority in the country of origin/export, which shows that there is no contamination of these goods when the shipment is for commercial use. Once a shipment surpasses 15 kg or is intended for sale, an import notification form has to be submitted at the Inspectorate VSA along with a health certificate from the country of origin, and these requirements are being strictly enforced at all points of entry. Live birds or poultry for breeding/restocking purposes have their own process: the Inspectorate TEATT–Unit LVV must be notified by the freight forwarders for all bird or poultry importations to inspect the shipments on site before giving clearance. For a commercial shipment of meat, poultry, or fish, our basic document set should be: commercial invoice, bill of lading/airway bill, packing list, a country-of-origin health certificate, and (above the 15 kg/commercial threshold) the import notification form filed with the Inspectorate VSA before or on arrival.

### Labeling & Standards
This is an area where Sint Maarten has historically been very easy on US suppliers, though the detail is a bit dated so we should double-check before a first shipment. Sint Maarten accepts U.S. labels and imposes no additional labeling requirements on U.S. products, remaining an open market for U.S. food and agricultural products with duty-free entry and limited import regulatory requirements. Sint Maarten has no nutrition-specific labeling requirements, accepts U.S. Recommended Daily Intake values, and labels are accepted in English, Dutch, Papiamento and Spanish — though authorities were at one point considering narrowing that to English and Dutch only. On quality and safety standards more broadly, Sint Maarten leans on international reference points rather than building everything from scratch: the local Inspectorate does not maintain its own maximum residue limits for pesticides and other contaminants in food products, instead relying on internationally accepted Codex standards and/or U.S. guidance. The lead domestic body for food products is the Ministry of Public Health, Social Development and Labour (VSA), which regulates pharmaceuticals, medical devices, cosmetics, and food products, including import control and public health monitoring , with the Bureau of Standards under Ministry TEATT handling broader product-standard oversight.

### Prohibited & Restricted Products
There's no blanket list of banned foods, but controls do exist and get enforced, and they can appear with little warning if there's a health scare. Prohibited and restricted goods, controlled substances, firearms, certain agricultural and food products, protected species and anything requiring a permit are all controlled at the Sint Maarten border. Free port status removes duty, not controls — plants, animals and animal products need permits or are restricted, and agricultural cargo can be inspected on arrival. The practical lesson for us is that Sint Maarten's Inspectorate can and does impose ad hoc, origin-specific bans in response to food-safety events: when the 2017 Brazilian "rotten meat" scandal broke, the Inspectorate imposed a temporary ban on all imports of Brazilian meat products and poultry meat from the companies implicated, in collaboration with the Department of Economics (TEZVT), to be lifted depending on developments in Brazil. So a clean, well-documented supply chain with traceability back to source isn't just good practice for us — it's our insurance against being caught up in a sudden restriction aimed at a different supplier.

### Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
For animal-origin products (meat, poultry, seafood, eggs), health certification is the core requirement, as covered above — a country-of-origin health certificate plus, above commercial thresholds, an import notification filed with Inspectorate VSA. For plant products like fresh produce and grains, the picture has historically been lighter but is evolving: a phytosanitary export certificate for plant products such as fresh produce was not presently required as of the most recent detailed report, but was expected to be required once anticipated plant protection legislation entered into force. Given that this reporting is several years old and Sint Maarten has been actively building out its own regulatory framework, our safest approach is to always have a phytosanitary certificate ready for produce and grain shipments even if it isn't always demanded at the border — it costs us little and protects us if the rules have tightened since the last published guidance. On the clearance side, the process itself tends to move fast for us: perishable goods have priority and usually are cleared within one day, dry goods can be cleared within 2-3 days, and the customs inspection is efficient and well-managed.

## Free Trade Agreements
This section is a bit counterintuitive, so it's worth spelling out clearly for our own planning. Sint Maarten does not have a reciprocal free trade agreement with the United States, and importantly, it isn't even a beneficiary of the main unilateral US Caribbean preference program anymore. Sint Maarten, which had previously been eligible for CBERA and CBTPA benefits as part of the Netherlands Antilles, did not seek to continue independently as a beneficiary. That program — the Caribbean Basin Initiative — is about duty-free access *into the US* for goods made in beneficiary countries; it was launched by the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act (CBERA) and as of 2025 provided 17 countries and dependent territories with duty-free access to the U.S. market for most goods — but Sint Maarten simply isn't one of them.

Here's why that doesn't actually hurt us: CBI-style preferences matter for goods flowing *from* Sint Maarten *into* the US. Since we're looking at this as a market to *sell into* from our US/other sourcing base, what matters to us is Sint Maarten's own border treatment of our goods coming in — and as covered above, that's already a unilateral 0% duty regime open to everyone, US included, with no FTA required to get the benefit. In other words, we don't need a trade agreement to get duty-free treatment in Sint Maarten; the island extends that to all origins by default as a free port. The absence of an FTA only becomes relevant if we ever wanted to source finished food products *from* Sint Maarten and re-export them to the US under preferential terms — and given the island has essentially no food manufacturing base, that's not a realistic path for us anyway.

Sint Maarten is also outside the EU customs/VAT area despite its Kingdom-of-the-Netherlands ties: it is outside the Schengen Area and the EU VAT Area, and Sint Maarten is not part of the EU or Eurozone. So if the Netherlands or wider EU market is ever part of our footprint, we shouldn't assume any EU internal-market mechanics apply to shipments destined for Sint Maarten — it has to be treated as its own distinct, non-EU customs territory.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
We found no evidence of seasonal import bans, harvest-protection windows, or licensing quotas tied to a domestic growing season — which tracks with everything else we know about this market. Sint Maarten has essentially no agricultural sector to protect: agriculture accounted for just 0.4% of GDP, against 18.3% industry and 81.3% services , and as noted earlier almost all food must be imported due to no significant agriculture and limited local fishing. With no meaningful harvest to shield, there's no policy rationale for seasonal gating the way there might be in a market with an active domestic produce or poultry sector — and we found nothing in current regulation suggesting otherwise. We'll keep an eye on this given the government's stated interest in "promoting local agriculture" as a resilience measure, but as of today there's no seasonal restriction regime to navigate.

## Protected / Reserved Products
We did not find any formal import-substitution policy, subsidy regime, or reserved-product list shielding specific domestic food categories from import competition. That's consistent with the underlying economics: with agriculture at roughly 0.4% of GDP and virtually no food manufacturing base, there isn't really a domestic industry of any scale left to protect through tariffs or quotas on our product categories (produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains, seafood). The one soft caveat is the policy direction flagged by the World Bank — promoting local agriculture and reduced import dependence is explicitly mentioned as part of the island's resilience strategy — so if any targeted protection measure ever emerges, it would most plausibly appear first around small-scale truck-farm produce or egg production rather than meat, grains, or seafood, which the island has no realistic capacity to produce at any scale.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
The import/export pattern here is about as asymmetric as it gets, and that asymmetry is the whole opportunity for us. On the import side, the main imports to St Maarten are cars, jewelry, diamonds, pearls, food products, petroleum, recreational boats and so on — food sits as one of the handful of true staple categories alongside fuel and consumer goods, not a niche line item. On the export side, there is essentially no food export story: the main exports from St Maarten are gold, sea vessels, furniture, scrap, aluminum and liquor — none of it agricultural or food-related. The Dutch side also plays a quiet re-export role for the island's northern, French half, since the main industries are food services or restaurants, hotels, airlines, transportation, entertainment, and cruise ships rather than production or processing of any kind.

What this tells us is straightforward: there is no competitive domestic food-export industry we're up against, and no meaningful two-way food trade to manage — this is a pure consumption market for us, overwhelmingly fed by imports, with demand concentrated in exactly the tourism/hospitality channel where consistent, high-quality meat, poultry, seafood, produce, and grain supply commands a premium. Our main competition isn't local producers; it's other import suppliers (historically a lot of US-origin product, per the FAIRS reporting) and whoever currently holds the hotel and cruise-provisioning contracts.

## Other Market Context
A few additional things worth knowing before we plan logistics and pricing. First, the currency: in Sint Maarten (Dutch south), the Netherlands Antillean guilder (ANG) was the official currency from 1940 until 2025, when it was replaced by the Caribbean Guilder (XCG), which is pegged to the United States dollar. The US dollar is also commonly used on both sides of the island , which is genuinely convenient for us as a USD-denominated business — currency risk and repatriation friction should be minimal compared with markets running on a freely floating local currency.

Second, the macro backdrop is currently favorable but not without risk. Sint Maarten's inflation is expected to rise to 2.8% in 2026, and while Curaçao and Sint Maarten are not directly exposed to most tariff measures, the indirect effects on tourism demand, imported inflation, investment, and global economic activity could affect the monetary union. Given how directly tourism volume translates into food-service demand for us, cruise and stay-over arrival trends are a leading indicator worth tracking as part of our account planning, more so than most macro indicators.

Third, on logistics: Sint Maarten's customs process is set up to move perishables quickly rather than hold them up, which matters a lot for anything in our chilled or frozen meat, poultry, seafood, and produce lines — fast perishable clearance (noted above) is a real operational advantage versus markets where chilled cargo sits in queue.

Finally, worth flagging that some of the most detailed US-sourced regulatory guidance we could find on labeling and phytosanitary specifics dates back roughly a decade, from a period when Sint Maarten, with its newly acquired status as an autonomous country, was beginning to develop its own food safety, and plant and animal health regulatory systems, with full implementation expected to take some time given the resource constraints of this young country. That means before any first shipment of a new product category, we should confirm current specifics directly with Inspectorate VSA / Ministry TEATT rather than relying solely on older published guidance — the general duty-free, import-friendly posture is clearly durable, but fine print on documentation may have shifted.
