# Bahamas — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: General customs duty averages 30-35% (range 0-210%), with food and beverage rates varying by product type
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 10% standard rate; 0% on unprepared essential foods as of April 1, 2026; 5% on certain other essential goods
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — Bahamas has no bilateral FTA with the US, only the CARICOM-US Trade and Investment Framework Agreement (2013) and one-way CBI/CBERA preferences
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes — spiny lobster (crawfish) closed season April 1-July 31, plus standing import licenses required for chicken, other poultry, meat, dairy and eggs
- Top imported food products: Poultry, beef, dairy products, fresh vegetables, prepared/snack foods
- Top exported food products: Spiny lobster (crawfish), conch, salt, rum

## Import Tariffs & Duties
Here's the big picture for us: Bahamas runs its government almost entirely off consumption and import taxes rather than income tax, so duty rates are a real cost line we need to build into our landed-cost math, not an afterthought. Officially, customs duty ranges from 0% to 210% and is collected on all imports, with an average rate of duty of 30% - 35% . Some more recent trade-compliance sources put the 2026-2027 range even wider — between 0% and 220%, with an average rate of duty of 5% to 35% — so the exact number really depends on the HS code, and we should always run a formal duty lookup before quoting a customer.

For our specific categories, duties on food and beverages vary, with canned goods and beverages generally taxed at higher rates than fresh produce . That's useful for us strategically — it suggests fresh/raw agricultural shipments (our bread and butter) tend to clear at friendlier rates than processed or packaged goods, which lines up with our direct-from-grower model rather than a value-added/packaged play for this market.

On top of the duty itself, every shipment picks up additional charges. There's a processing fee of 1%, with a minimum of $10 and a maximum of $1,500 , plus an environmental levy line item on the official duty calculator. Some sources cite a lower cap on the processing fee (around $750), so we should confirm the current cap with a broker or the Click2Clear portal before finalizing pricing. Duties and fees are all calculated the same way: customs duties are levied at different rates on imported goods based on their tariff headings in the Schedules to the Customs Management Act, calculated on the cost, insurance, and freight (CIF) value of the goods at the time of importation — so freight and insurance costs directly inflate our duty bill, which matters for how we structure quotes.

Practically, clearance requires the standard document set — commercial invoice, bill of lading/airway bill, packing list, and the customs declaration form — and a Certificate of Origin is necessary to claim preferential duty rates under specific trade agreements like the EPA or CARICOM . If we ever route product through a CARICOM or EU/UK-origin supply chain, that document is what unlocks the lower rate — worth building into our supplier paperwork requirements from day one.

## Taxes
The VAT picture in Bahamas has shifted significantly in our favor over the past year, and this is genuinely good news for us as a food supplier. The standard VAT rate is 10%, down from 12%, signalling a policy shift toward targeted tax relief rather than uniform rate reductions . But the headline change for us is the food-specific relief: the Bahamas applies a 0% VAT rate to unprepared and essential food items from 1 April 2026 . More specifically, the zero-rating applies to unprepared food items commonly purchased in grocery stores, including fresh fruit and vegetables, baby food, lunch snacks, frozen foods, and other everyday staples, which had already benefited from a reduced VAT rate of 5%, introduced in 2025 . Crucially for us, this isn't just a retail-shelf benefit — the exemption applies to both wholesale and retail transactions, as well as imports , so our bulk shipments of fresh produce, seafood, meat and similar unprepared goods should clear without VAT being layered on top of duty, directly improving our margin math versus prior years.

For goods that don't fall into the zero-rated basket, certain essential goods, such as medical and hygiene products, are taxed at a reduced VAT rate of 5% , and everything else defaults to the 10% standard rate. On the business side, if we ever set up a local entity or distributor relationship, note that businesses offering taxable supplies are required to register for VAT when their total taxable revenue meets or exceeds 100,000 Bahamian dollars (BSD) within any 12-month period . Also worth flagging: excise tax exists separately for products like alcohol, tobacco and petroleum, with excise taxes levied at varying rates on specific manufactured products, spanning from 5% to 300% — relevant if we ever add wine/spirits or similar lines, but not something that touches our core produce/protein business.

## Free Trade Agreements
This is the section that matters most for how we think about Bahamas relative to our other main market (the US), and the honest answer is: there's no FTA to lean on. The Bahamas does not have a free trade agreement with the United States, but it is a signatory to the U.S.-CARICOM Trade and Investment Framework Agreement (2013), which provides a platform for dialogue on trade and investment issues — that's a forum for talking, not a tariff-cutting mechanism we can invoke on an invoice.

What Bahamas does have is one-way preferential access into other markets, which mostly helps Bahamian exporters selling out, not us selling in. Bahamas is a member of CARICOM, a beneficiary under the US Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI) and CBERA, and Canada's CARIBCAN Program, and is party to the EU-CARIFORUM and UK-CARIFORUM Economic Partnership Agreements, which provide preferential access for goods produced in The Bahamas to most major markets . In plain terms: if we ever export Bahamian-origin lobster or conch onward to the US or EU, that Bahamian origin can catch preferential treatment; but there is no reciprocal duty relief for our shipments going into Bahamas from the US.

The practical upshot for us as an importer into Bahamas: outside of the Dominican Republic-Central America Free Trade Agreement (CAFTA-DR), Caribbean nations do not have bilateral free trade agreements with the United States, only having access to U.S. markets under the CBI . Also worth knowing: The Bahamas is not a member of the World Trade Organization (WTO) and does not offer export subsidies, engage in trade-distorting practices, or maintain a formal local content requirement (though see the informal 40% policy in Protected/Reserved Products below). Despite the lack of an FTA, the market is still wide open to us in practice — the United States maintains a $3.8 billion trade surplus with The Bahamas, which imports 80-90 percent of its food and fuel from the United States . So we're not fighting for shelf space against cheaper FTA-protected competitors; we're mainly competing against other US-based suppliers on price, quality, and reliability, which plays to our direct-sourcing model.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
There isn't a broad seasonal embargo on food imports generally, but there are two specific things we need on our calendar. First, on the export side (if we ever buy Bahamian seafood to sell into our other markets): April 1st marks the start of the annual closed season for the Bahamian spiny lobster (crawfish) fishery, which continues until July 31 , and taking, selling or buying crawfish during the closed season is illegal . Given that Bahamian lobster exports generate close to $100 million annually and the spiny lobster fishery makes up 80% of the country's total fisheries income , this is the single most important seasonal fact for us if crawfish/lobster ever becomes part of our two-way trade with Bahamas — we simply cannot source or move product from that fishery for a third of the year.

Second, on the import side: poultry (specifically chicken) requires standing licensing, not seasonal but worth flagging here since it functions as a year-round gate. No person shall import any chicken (fowl) and chicken parts into The Bahamas without a licence issued by the Minister , and more broadly a permit is required to import poultry, meat, and eggs . This isn't a closed window like the lobster season, but it does mean every poultry shipment needs a live, approved license and sanitary certificate lined up before it ships — something our logistics team needs to build lead time around.

## Protected / Reserved Products
Bahamas is actively trying to grow its own agriculture and fisheries sector, and that's shaping policy in ways we should watch closely. The government aims to increase the agriculture and fisheries contribution to GDP from $150 million (1.5%) to $1.2 billion (10%) by 2031, with policies encouraging foreign investors to source locally . The clearest expression of this is a local-content push aimed at our natural customer base (hotels and resorts): to enhance food security, promote economic diversification, and ensure market access for local producers, the Bahamian government recently announced foreign investors must purchase 40 percent of all food intended for operations from local farmers and fishers . Importantly for us, this policy is not yet supported by legislation, although several international companies have voiced support as part of their corporate social responsibility initiatives — so it's currently a soft/voluntary target rather than a hard quota, but it's clearly the direction of travel and we should expect it to firm up over time.

This also connects to a regional target that predates the current push: in alignment with CARICOM's goal to cut extra-regional food imports by 25 percent by 2025, the government previously introduced an informal policy encouraging a 40 percent local content requirement in Heads of Agreement . What this means for us practically: our institutional targets (cruise lines, hotels, resorts) may increasingly face internal or government pressure to source a meaningful share of produce, seafood, meat and eggs locally. Our best play is likely to lean into this rather than fight it — positioning ourselves as a complement to local sourcing for the categories Bahamas can't reliably grow or catch at scale (grains, most meat and poultry, and much of the produce that doesn't suit the islands' soil and water constraints), rather than trying to compete head-on for the same short list of products the government is trying to protect (beef/pork, dairy, winter vegetables, mariculture/shrimp are explicitly flagged as areas the government wants to build domestically).

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
This is a category where we need real operational discipline, because Bahamas' SPS system is document-heavy even though it's fairly receptive to US-standard products. For poultry specifically, BAHFSA has done the regulatory homework already: an import risk analysis recommends that the importation of poultry and poultry products to The Bahamas could be permitted subject to compliance with Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) measures to manage the quarantine risks of a range of significant diseases to a very low level, in line with WOAH guidelines , covering diseases like avian influenza, Newcastle disease and Gumboro disease . Every poultry consignment needs a valid import permit with Conditions for Import as an Annex, accompanied by a Sanitary Certificate from the source in the Country of Origin .

For fresh produce, phytosanitary certificates from the country of origin must accompany imported fresh produce and plants , and for any live animals or animal products, health certificates must accompany live animals . Seafood has its own permitting track: the Department of Marine Resources issues the import permits for seafood products, and import permits can usually be obtained within 48 hours — genuinely fast turnaround for a perishable category, which works in our favor logistically.

A few things simplify our compliance burden compared to some other markets: like the majority of Caribbean nations, The Bahamas does not require facility registration of foreign establishments or sanitary registration of food products , and The Bahamas has no quarantine facilities — meaning product either clears on paper or it doesn't; there's no holding-pen process to plan around. On labeling, the rules are US-friendly in substance: no person shall offer for sale in The Bahamas any package of food unless the label is written in English, specifies the product name, specifies the date of manufacture and expiration, permits traceability of the food as prescribed in regulations, and sets out such particulars as may be prescribed in regulations . And for meat/poultry specifically sourced from the US, products from the United States must have the USDA inspection seal and must be from a federally inspected U.S. facility — something our US-side suppliers should already be positioned to provide without extra cost to us.

The bottom line: Bahamas trusts US-origin certification heavily, which is a real advantage for us given our existing US supplier network — we're not being asked to build a parallel compliance system, just to keep the standard USDA/phytosanitary paperwork flowing consistently with every shipment.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
The trade pattern here tells us exactly where we fit. On the import side, of the $4.9 billion in commodities imported into The Bahamas in 2024, Food & Live Animals accounted for 17%, or $815 million — a massive, food-import-dependent economy by design, since the islands simply can't grow enough to feed themselves or their tourism base. The Bahamas imports nearly 90 percent of its food, at an annual cost of approximately $1 billion, with U.S. suppliers dominating given consumer preference for American brands and direct procurement by large resorts . The specific categories US exporters are already winning tell us where the demand is proven: poultry meat and products, beef and beef products, dairy products, fats and oil products, snack food, prepared food, fruit and vegetable juices, pork and pork products, wine and beer, and fresh vegetables . Growth categories to watch in 2024 included fresh fruit and vegetables, distilled spirits, beer, eggs and products, milk substitutes, microwave popcorn, soybean oil, sausages, beef and products, and pork and products .

On the export side, Bahamas' domestic food exports are narrow but valuable, and dominated by seafood: the main category of domestic exports was food and live animals, valued at USD 90.1 million, or 49% of domestic exports , with major exports including crustaceans (particularly lobster), salt, and rum , mostly headed to the United States and Europe .

What this tells us strategically: Bahamas is overwhelmingly a one-way sourcing opportunity for us on the sell side — a market to ship US and Latin American-origin protein, grains, produce and eggs into, not a market with a deep and diverse export base we'd rely on for inbound supply (lobster/crawfish being the clear exception, and even that comes with a four-month closed season). The institutional buyer base we already target — cruise lines, hotels, resorts, food distributors — is exactly who's driving this import demand, since the Caribbean hotel, restaurant, institutional (HRI) foodservice sector accounts for roughly 30% of consumer-oriented agricultural imports . That's a direct match to our existing target market list and sales process — we don't need to build new customer relationships from scratch, just extend the ones we're already building elsewhere in the Caribbean.

## Other Market Context
A few structural things make Bahamas a comparatively easy market for us to transact in financially, even with import costs factored in. The Bahamian Government maintains the value of the Bahamian dollar on a par with the U.S. dollar — a hard 1:1 peg, which means we don't carry currency/FX risk on Bahamian-dollar-denominated transactions the way we might elsewhere in Latin America, and pricing in USD terms is straightforward. On the tax side beyond VAT and duty, there is no personal income tax in the Bahamas, and also no capital gains tax, no net wealth tax and no inheritance, estate or gift tax — relevant if we ever set up a local entity or joint venture rather than just shipping in as an exporter.

Macro conditions look stable enough to support continued food demand: unemployment is expected to remain stable at just below 10 percent through 2026, while The Bahamas' GDP growth prospects for this year have been revised upwards again to 2.8 percent . The government's fiscal position is tighter, though — central government debt remains elevated at 74 percent of GDP and further fiscal consolidation is required — which is part of why we're seeing VAT/duty policy tinkering (the food VAT relief) alongside broader revenue pressure; the IMF has even floated a much higher VAT rate as an austerity option, though the government has publicly rejected that. Retail demand itself is healthy: grocery retail sales topped US$1.2 billion in 2024, with supermarkets accounting for US$389.7 million of that amount , concentrated heavily around Nassau where most of the population and our institutional targets sit.

On logistics, most of the country's food comes in through Nassau, but the Bahamas' Freeport Container Port and oil bunkering operations contribute to re-export activity — worth keeping in mind if Freeport/Grand Bahama becomes a useful secondary gateway as we scale, especially since it has separate import procedures worth checking with customs. And administratively, note that operating commercially in-market requires compliance with the Business License Act — goods imported for the purpose of any business are restricted unless the importer produces for inspection a current Business License issued under the provisions of The Business License Act — so if we ever move from arm's-length exporting to a more embedded local presence, that's a prerequisite step.
