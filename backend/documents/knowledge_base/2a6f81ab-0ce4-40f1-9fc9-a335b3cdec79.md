# Trinidad and Tobago — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts

- Average food-import tariff: MFN applied simple average of 19.0% on agricultural products (2023), though rates run from 0% to 45% with most general goods at 20%
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 12.5% standard rate, but basic food items and agricultural supplies are zero-rated
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — no comprehensive FTA; T&T exports benefit from unilateral U.S. Caribbean Basin Initiative preferences, but U.S. goods entering T&T get no special tariff break
- Seasonal import restrictions: No formal seasonal calendar found; a standing year-round import-license regime restricts several ag/seafood items to protect local producers
- Top imported food products: cereals and cereal preparations, dairy products and eggs, meat and meat preparations, fruits and vegetables
- Top exported food products: bottled water, prepared cereal foods, aromatic bitters, non-alcoholic beverages, cocoa/chocolate preparations, sauces and condiments

## Import Tariffs & Duties

If we're bringing product into Trinidad and Tobago, we need to plan for real variability in duty rates depending on category — this isn't a flat-tariff market. At the macro level, Trinidad and Tobago's simple average MFN applied tariff was 8.2% overall in 2023, but 19.0% specifically on agricultural products versus 6.4% on non-agricultural goods . That gap matters a lot for us since virtually everything we'd ship — produce, meat, poultry, eggs, grains, seafood — falls on the higher-tariff agricultural side. The trade-weighted numbers tell a similar story: the trade-weighted average tariff was 8.1% overall, but 20.0% for agricultural goods in 2023 .

At the product-schedule level, tariff rates in Trinidad and Tobago range from zero to 45 percent, but most goods are set at 20 percent . On top of straight ad valorem duties, T&T's customs regime layers in a few different calculation methods we should be ready for: customs duties are applied as 12.5 percent ad valorem calculated on customs value, a specific duty calculated by a determined value per commodity unit, or a composite duty combining ad valorem and specific methods . Practically, this means before we quote any customer or lock in a supply contract, we need to pull the exact HS code for each SKU rather than assuming a blanket rate — a container of frozen chicken and a container of fresh mangoes will not be taxed the same way, and won't even use the same calculation method.

Poultry deserves a special mention since it's one of our core categories. Industry reporting has flagged that Trinidad and Tobago applies a Common External Tariff of 40 percent with a 15 percent surcharge on poultry, and — unlike Barbados and Jamaica — does not operate a tariff-rate quota system . That's a meaningfully higher duty than the "most goods at 20%" baseline, so if poultry is part of our T&T play, we should budget for a combined ~55% duty burden on top of CIF value, not the generic 20%.

All of this is calculated the same way regardless of category: Trinidad and Tobago calculates duties using the CIF method, meaning import duty and taxes are based on the value of goods plus shipping costs . For us, that means freight and insurance aren't just logistics costs — they directly inflate our duty bill, so tight freight-cost management has a double payoff here.

The most reliable way for us to nail down exact, current rates before we commit to a shipment is T&T's own government tool: the Tariff Finder allows searching for Most-Favoured-Nation tariff rates for commodities imported into Trinidad and Tobago, with all commodities classified under the CARICOM External Tariff system compliant with the international Harmonized System . We should build a habit of checking each new SKU there rather than relying on general averages.

## Taxes

The good news for us on the tax side is that Trinidad and Tobago treats basic food and agricultural products favorably under VAT. The standard VAT rate applicable to commercial supplies is 12.5%, but basic food items and agricultural supplies are zero-rated, as are crude oil, natural gas, and exported goods and services . That zero-rating is a real win for our margins if our product lines qualify — it means no 12.5% tax stacking on top of duty for a lot of what we'd be moving, though we'll want to confirm SKU-by-SKU which of our lines count as "basic food" versus more processed or "specialty" items that might not qualify for the zero rate.

For context on the broader system: Trinidad and Tobago levies a flat 25% personal income tax rate, a 30% corporate tax rate (with a 35% rate for petrochemical companies), and a 12.5% value-added tax . If we ever set up a local entity or joint venture in T&T rather than just exporting into it, the 30% corporate tax rate is the number to plan around. Also worth flagging for any local partner or distributor we work with: the VAT registration threshold for companies making commercial supplies is TTD 600,000 for a 12-month period — useful to know if we're structuring a local distribution arrangement rather than direct import.

## Free Trade Agreements

This is the section where we need to manage expectations, because there is no comprehensive U.S.–Trinidad and Tobago free trade agreement that lowers duties on our goods going into T&T. What exists instead is a patchwork of preference programs that mostly work in the *other* direction — helping T&T-origin goods enter the U.S., not the reverse.

The main vehicle is the Caribbean Basin Initiative. The Caribbean Basin Initiative remains a vital element in U.S. economic relations with Central America and the Caribbean, intended to facilitate economic development and export diversification of Caribbean Basin economies, and initially launched in 1983 through the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act and substantially expanded in 2000 through the U.S.-Caribbean Basin Trade Partnership Act, currently providing beneficiary countries with duty-free access to the U.S. market for most goods . This is genuinely useful for us if we ever want to source from Trinidad and Tobago and sell into our U.S. customer base — T&T-grown or processed products can often move into the U.S. duty-free under this program. Historically T&T has been a strong user of it too: Trinidad and Tobago has been the leading exporter to the United States under CBERA, exporting $551.8 million in 2018, up 13.1 percent from 2017 .

There's also an ongoing government-to-government dialogue layer: the United States and CARICOM have maintained the United States–CARICOM Trade and Investment Council, a discussion forum for multilateral issues ranging from intellectual property and trade preference programs to broader regional concerns , and agricultural trade specifically comes up there — the delegations have discussed agricultural sustainability and food security, areas where CARICOM and the United States share common interests and objectives .

A recent, very relevant development for us: in late 2025, Trinidad and Tobago secured a carve-out from the broader U.S. tariff actions for its agricultural exports. The Ministry of Foreign and CARICOM Affairs confirmed that Trinidad and Tobago will benefit from zero reciprocal tariffs on several qualifying agricultural products, including critical nitrogen and phosphate fertilizers, entering the United States , following a comprehensive strategy adopted since the imposition of global U.S. tariffs earlier in 2025, with several high-level engagements held over four months to address the issue directly with senior U.S. officials . Again, this is about T&T exports reaching the U.S. more cheaply — good news if we're using T&T as a sourcing hub for our U.S.-facing business, not a reason to expect cheaper duties on what we ship into T&T.

On the regional side, T&T's core trade architecture is CARICOM. Trinidad and Tobago actively leverages regional integration through CARICOM alongside strategic trade deals with European and Latin American partners, with these agreements significantly eliminating tariffs on qualifying goods, and Trinidad and Tobago utilizing the CARICOM Common External Tariff structure that assesses duties on the CIF value of imported goods . So if we're competing against CARICOM-origin suppliers (say, produce from Guyana or Jamaica), they likely clear customs duty-free or at preferential rates where we won't — that's a real competitive gap we should factor into pricing.

Bottom line for us: there's no tariff shortcut on the import side. If we're shipping into T&T from the U.S. or elsewhere outside CARICOM, we pay the standard MFN/CET rates described above. The trade-agreement upside is specifically on the export leg — if we source T&T products for our U.S. customer network, CBI-style preferences can make that flow duty-free on the U.S. end.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans

We didn't find a published seasonal calendar (e.g., "no imports of X between these months to protect local harvest") for Trinidad and Tobago. What we did find is something that functions similarly in practice: a standing, year-round licensing regime that restricts a specific list of agricultural and seafood products regardless of season. Section 45 of the Customs Act requires an import license for a list of items including coconut in all forms (seedlings, copra, desiccated coconut, coconut milk and cream), fresh fish (live or dead), chilled or frozen shrimp, lobster and crabmeat, live poultry, and oilseeds, beans, and nuts . For us, that means several of our core categories — live poultry and certain shellfish in particular — aren't a simple "pay duty and ship" proposition; we need a license from the relevant ministry lined up well before the goods arrive, or they get held at the port.

Plant-origin products carry their own permitting layer on every shipment, not just seasonally. No person may import into Trinidad and Tobago any fruits, planting material, plant pests, pathogens, plant products, soil, vegetables, or other prescribed "restricted articles" without authorization , and applications for a Plant Import Permit are submitted electronically via the e-permits and Licenses module on the TTbizlink online applications platform . So for our fresh produce and herbs lines, this permit is a standard pre-shipment step for every order, not an occasional hurdle.

Poultry and eggs are the category where we should expect the most active, protectionist attention, even if it isn't calendar-based. The Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries has been pressing ahead with measures to strengthen the poultry sector, including a proposed Public-Private Partnership to establish a large-scale poultry breeding and hatchery facility , explicitly aimed at reducing the sector's reliance on imported inputs, strengthening biosecurity and food security, and conserving foreign exchange . This tells us the direction of travel — T&T wants to import less poultry over time, not more, so we shouldn't build a long-term plan around T&T being a growth market for bulk chicken exports from us.

On the animal-health side, disease-related restrictions do function like ad hoc "bans," and it's worth knowing T&T's current approach isn't a blanket shutdown. The detection of avian influenza in a country does not automatically trigger a blanket ban on imports from that nation — instead, Trinidad and Tobago applies science-based, risk-based measures in line with World Organization for Animal Health standards, including zoning arrangements that permit imports from approved disease-free areas . Practically, that means if there's an HPAI outbreak in one of our supplier regions, we may still be able to ship from unaffected zones with the right veterinary paperwork — worth confirming case-by-case with T&T's Ministry of Agriculture rather than assuming an automatic shutout. There is a specific standing rule to know, though: effective April 24, 2024, Trinidad and Tobago will not accept live poultry shipments originating from or transiting through the 10km zone surrounding an HPAI detection .

## Protected / Reserved Products

Poultry and table eggs are clearly the most politically protected food category in T&T, and this is the one area where we should expect real friction as a foreign supplier. Local industry groups have been vocal: extra-regional poultry imports continue to threaten domestic production, especially frozen poultry, with producers in T&T, Jamaica, Belize and Guyana particularly vulnerable to cheap imports, and low-quality poultry imports undermining local production and compromising health standards . If we're pitching poultry into T&T, we should expect scrutiny, price competition from a well-organized local lobby, and government policy actively trying to shrink our addressable market over time rather than grow it.

Coconut is a smaller but interesting case — it's fully import-restricted rather than merely tariffed, requiring a license under the Customs Act as noted above, which strongly suggests it's treated as a protected/import-substitution product tied to local coconut cultivation.

Cocoa is the reverse situation for us — it's a product T&T is trying to rebuild as an export strength rather than protect as an import-substitute, which matters if we ever want to source from T&T rather than sell into it. Despite a more than 400-year history of exporting cocoa beans with a reputation for quality, cocoa production fell from 30,000 to 500 tonnes per annum over the past century, and in August 2015 a local firm launched the country's first cocoa processing plant with a capacity of 50 tonnes per year . That's a potential niche sourcing opportunity for us on the export side of our business, not a competitive threat on the import side.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements

The overall posture toward U.S.-standard food products is encouraging, but the details matter for how we manage new product launches. Trinidad and Tobago is fairly receptive to imports of U.S. agricultural products; initially the level of scrutiny and checks on imported products can be quite intense, but once an importer demonstrates a successful track record of compliance, the import flow becomes much smoother . This tells us to expect the first few shipments of any new product line to face heavier inspection than shipment twenty — worth building extra buffer time and paperwork rigor into our first few orders with any new T&T customer or new SKU.

New-to-market products get extra attention specifically: the government prefers to review new-to-market items before allowing entry, laboratory testing may be required at the government's discretion, and in such cases products are usually detained until laboratory results become available . For us, that's a real planning risk for anything perishable — if we're launching a new fresh produce or seafood line, we should assume possible lab-hold delays and price that risk into our first shipments (or pilot with air freight/small volumes rather than a full container).

Labeling compliance is enforced at multiple points, not just at the border: enforcement of labeling requirements is carried out mostly at the port of entry but routine and random checks at the retail and wholesale levels are also conducted . That means getting labeling right on day one protects us even after goods have cleared customs.

On permits and certificates specifically, T&T runs distinct licensing lanes by product type: import/export permits authorize the importation of live animals and animal products, are required to export dairy-based goods derived from animals, are required to export meat and poultry products from Trinidad and Tobago, and allow the import of plants and plant products under strict phytosanitary conditions . For our seafood, meat, poultry, and egg lines specifically, this means engaging the Ministry of Agriculture's veterinary/import-permit process well ahead of shipment — not something we can handle purely through a customs broker at the port.

The good overall summary for our category mix comes from USDA's own market assessment: U.S. suppliers willing to clear initial hurdles will find Trinidad and Tobago to be an import-friendly country allowing for relatively smooth flowing movement of food products through its borders, though special attention needs to be given to licensing, certification, labeling and documentation for select products to avoid customs clearance delays . That matches our overall read: this is a workable market for us, but one where documentation discipline is the difference between smooth flow and repeated delays.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products

T&T's food trade pattern is a pretty clean opportunity map for us. On the import side, the country is structurally dependent on food imports because of its small landmass and high average income, and the categories are heavily concentrated: Trinidad and Tobago is dependent on food imports given its relatively small land area and high average income, with cereals and cereal preparations, fruits and vegetables, dairy products and birds' eggs, and meat and meat preparations traditionally accounting for approximately 80% of the food import bill . That's almost a direct match to our own product categories (grains, produce, dairy/eggs, meat/poultry), which is encouraging — we're not trying to force our catalog into a market that doesn't want it.

More recent, granular data backs this up from the U.S. side specifically, which matters since the U.S. is T&T's largest supplier. In 2024, the top U.S. agricultural exports to Trinidad and Tobago included poultry meat and products at $31.26 million, eggs and products at $26.92 million, dairy products at $26.89 million, baked goods at $26.18 million, other feeds/meals/fodders at $24.81 million, wheat at $23.81 million, soybean meal, corn, fresh fruit, and pork and pork products . That's essentially a shopping list that overlaps almost entirely with our own catalog — grains, poultry, eggs, dairy, meat, and fresh fruit are already proven categories moving in volume from the U.S. into T&T, which tells us there's an established appetite and supply chain pattern we can plug into rather than having to create demand from scratch.

On the export side, T&T's strengths are almost entirely processed/value-added food and beverage products rather than raw agricultural commodities — a useful data point if we ever want T&T as a sourcing market rather than just a selling market. Exports in the food and beverage sector were valued at TT$3.1 billion in 2022, with the top exported commodities being water, pre-cooked and prepared food from cereals, aromatic bitters, other non-alcoholic beverages, and chocolate and cocoa preparations . In other words, T&T doesn't export much in the way of fresh produce, meat, or seafood — its comparative advantage is in bottling, processing, and branded consumer goods (Angostura bitters being the obvious flagship). That confirms our read that T&T is primarily a **selling** market for our fresh/wholesale categories, not a competing or sourcing market for the same goods — the competitive pressure we'd face there is from other importers (U.S., CARICOM, Latin American) targeting the same retail and institutional buyers, not from T&T's own farmers in categories like grain, dairy, or general produce. The one clear exception is poultry, where — as covered above — local producers are organized, protected, and explicitly trying to displace importers like us over time.

## Other Market Context

The single biggest operational risk we should have on our radar for Trinidad and Tobago isn't tariffs or SPS paperwork — it's foreign exchange access. This has been a chronic, worsening problem, not a one-off event. The IMF has warned that Trinidad and Tobago will need stricter control of government spending and higher interest rates to maintain its fixed currency, as reserves fall, with gross official reserves dropping from US$6,880 million in 2021 to US$5,369 million in 2025 and projected to fall further to US$4,607 million in 2026 — about 5.4 months of import cover . For an import-dependent economy, that's a thin cushion.

This shows up directly as a problem for companies like ours trying to get paid or move money. Access to foreign exchange remains the biggest challenge to bilateral trade with T&T — due to an overvalued currency and excess demand for foreign exchange, smaller local companies and those that don't generate their own U.S. dollars through exports often face a three-to-nine-month delay in acquiring foreign exchange to pay U.S. suppliers . That's a critical planning point for us: if our T&T buyer is a smaller distributor rather than a large hotel chain or cruise line with its own dollar-earning capacity, we could be looking at payment delays measured in months, not weeks — which affects our cash flow, credit terms, and how we structure contracts (e.g., requiring upfront deposits or working through buyers who already generate their own USD, like cruise lines and hotels).

The scale of the shortage has been serious enough to threaten basic food supply chains. Trinidad and Tobago faced a shortage of basic food items as wholesalers who import the majority of these goods were unable to access foreign exchange from the Exim Bank for two months , and the strain has pushed some businesses toward informal channels, where it is common to see importers indicate a willingness to pay a premium exchange rate versus what banks may offer . For us, this reinforces why our institutional-contract, larger-account strategy (cruise lines, hotels, resorts, food distributors) is the right fit for T&T specifically — those buyers are far more likely to have reliable dollar access than a smaller independent wholesaler would.

On the macro backdrop, demand conditions are stable if unspectacular: the IMF estimated the economy expanded by 0.8 percent in 2025, with inflation around 2 percent and unemployment below 5 percent, and the banking system described as well capitalised and resilient . That's not a booming market, but it's not a shrinking one either — steady enough to support our institutional-contract model, especially as cruise and hospitality volumes (a core part of our target customer base) continue recovering regionally.

Finally, on trading relationships generally: the U.S. is by far T&T's largest trading partner, which works in our favor culturally and logistically. Strong political, economic, and cultural ties with the United States give U.S. food and beverage products a competitive advantage , reinforced by frequent advertisement of U.S. food and beverage products on cable television, which maintains their popularity among Trinidad and Tobago consumers . On the flip side, we should go in with eyes open that local wholesalers already carry many major U.S. and international brands, making it difficult for new products to compete with these brands, and hard to find a local importer who doesn't already carry competing brands — a good reminder that our value proposition into this market needs to be about direct-sourcing economics and reliability, not just "we're American and therefore new."
