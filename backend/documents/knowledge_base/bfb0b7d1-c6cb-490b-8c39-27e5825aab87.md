# Saint Martin (French part) — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: Effectively 0% — Saint-Martin operates under a historic "free port" (port franc) customs regime
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 0% VAT; local 4% TGCA turnover tax applies instead on retail sales
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — no comprehensive US-EU FTA exists; Saint-Martin's zero-duty status comes from its free-port regime, not a bilateral trade deal
- Seasonal import restrictions: Not found — no specific seasonal bans/quotas identified for French Saint-Martin
- Top imported food products: Meat, dairy, cereal-based products, general packaged foodstuffs (nearly all food is imported)
- Top exported food products: Not found — local agriculture is under 1% of GDP, so food exports are negligible/not tracked

## Import Tariffs & Duties
Here's the headline for your business: bringing food and agricultural products *into* French Saint-Martin is, in practice, duty-free. This is one of the most distinctive — and advantageous — features of this market. Although Saint-Martin is technically part of the EU's customs territory as an "outermost region" (RUP), it has historically operated as a **port franc (free port)**, and French customs confirms this special treatment still applies today: the collectivity is an outermost region of the EU and sits within the EU customs territory, but it benefits from a provision allowing EU customs rules not to be fully applied there due to "customary practices of limited geographic and economic scope." The practical result, as French customs states plainly, is that Saint-Martin historically had free-port status, meaning goods imported into the territory did not pay customs duty , and a French Senate report on the two northern islands confirms this is still the practice today, noting that the northern islands are treated as customary free zones under the EU customs code, per a French declaration made before the code's adoption .

What this means for you: whether you're sourcing meat, poultry, eggs, produce, grains, or seafood from the US, from mainland France/EU, or from other Caribbean suppliers, you generally won't be paying an import tariff to bring it onto the island itself. That's a real cost advantage compared to shipping the same goods into a full EU customs territory like Guadeloupe or Martinique, where standard EU tariffs would apply (the EU's general common external tariff averages around 4.2%, ranging 0–17%, with duties ranging from 0-17%, with the general tariff averaging 4.2% , and foodstuffs specifically still carrying some protective tariff quotas under the EU's Common Agricultural Policy, the sectors of fabrics and items of clothing and foodstuffs still experience protection measures ).

One important catch to flag for your planning: this zero-duty treatment is specific to goods *staying* in Saint-Martin. If you ever plan to use Saint-Martin as a transshipment hub and move goods onward to Guadeloupe, Martinique, or mainland France, French customs is explicit that goods imported into or acquired on the island that are then exported to Guadeloupe, other overseas departments, or mainland France become subject to the taxes normally due upon arrival there — meaning they get treated as a fresh import at that second destination, with VAT and octroi de mer (a local import levy in those DOMs) applying at that point.

## Taxes
This is genuinely good news for a food importer: Saint-Martin does not apply the EU's VAT system at all. As the collectivity's own tax authority states, the value added tax (TVA) system existing within the European Union is not applicable to Saint-Martin . Instead, there's a local substitute: a turnover tax called the "general turnover tax" (TGCA), at a rate of 4%, applies in practice to retail sales of goods as well as all services . Structurally it behaves a lot like VAT — it uses concepts close to those governing VAT with regard to scope of application, taxable persons, and territoriality rules — but importantly, it's neutral for companies on retail sales, since businesses simply collect it from customers and remit it to the collectivity's public accountant .

The practical takeaway for a wholesale importer/distributor: if you're selling B2B (say, to a restaurant, hotel, or another distributor) rather than direct retail, you may fall outside the retail-sale trigger for TGCA — but if you're the one making the final retail sale to a consumer or non-resale business, you're the one who has to collect and remit the 4%. Compare that to mainland France's 20% standard VAT or Guadeloupe/Martinique's 8.5% — Saint-Martin's overall tax burden on food commerce is meaningfully lighter, which matters both for your pricing competitiveness and for cash-flow planning (no VAT recovery/input-credit mechanics to manage on this side of your business).

## Free Trade Agreements
Here's an important nuance for your US-facing business: Saint-Martin (French part) is **not** the same animal as Sint Maarten (Dutch side) when it comes to EU trade status, and this trips people up constantly. Sint Maarten is an Overseas Country and Territory (OCT), only loosely tied to the EU legal framework, whereas Saint Martin is an outermost region (RUP) of the EU — meaning EU law generally applies on the French side, and Saint-Martin (as confirmed by its acceptance of ATA carnets) is treated as EU territory: as an Overseas Collectivity of France, it is a member of the European Union and accepts ATA Carnets .

What does that mean for trading with the US specifically? There is no comprehensive US-EU free trade agreement (talks like TTIP never concluded), so in formal terms, US-origin goods entering EU customs territory would normally face the EU's standard MFN tariffs. However — and this is the key point for you — because Saint-Martin operates its historic free-port regime described above, that formal EU tariff schedule isn't actually being applied to your imports on the island itself. So in effect, you get duty-free access for US-sourced food and agricultural products without needing any FTA to get you there. The tradeoff is that this favorable treatment is a matter of French/EU administrative tolerance for a "territory of limited economic scope," not a locked-in treaty right — so it's worth monitoring for any future EU customs modernization efforts that might tighten this status, and it doesn't help you at all once goods move past Saint-Martin into full EU customs territory (Guadeloupe, Martinique, mainland France), where standard EU tariffs and rules of origin would apply.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
No current information found for seasonal bans, quotas, or licensing windows specific to French Saint-Martin. This is actually consistent with what we know about the territory: the agricultural sector represents less than 1% of Saint-Martin's GDP, less than 1% of jobs, and less than 1% of businesses registered with the local chamber of commerce — a sharp contrast to islands like Martinique or Guadeloupe, which run seasonal import protections around their banana, sugar, or produce harvests. Saint-Martin simply doesn't have a large enough domestic harvest calendar to justify that kind of protectionist machinery. That said, if you're also shipping through Guadeloupe or Martinique as part of your supply chain, be aware those DOMs *do* run seasonal restrictions on some produce categories — worth a separate check if that's part of your routing.

## Protected / Reserved Products
The one area where you should expect some friction is **locally produced meat**, which the Collectivité is actively trying to rebuild and protect. The island's only abattoir, run by the public body EGEA, reopened in October 2022 after a long closure, and it's a genuine local-industry priority: the Collectivité provided €70,000 in startup funding and €130,000 for operations and investment to get the facility its sanitary approval and back into operation . The EU has also chipped in significant development money — the Collectivité committed to €1,675,210 in renovation work co-financed by the EU's FEADER fund — to expand cutting and packaging capacity so the abattoir can process local beef, goat, sheep, and pork to European standards, ready for sale .

The Collectivité backs this up with enforcement: illegal slaughter (i.e., bypassing the official abattoir) is punishable by up to 6 months' imprisonment and a €7,000 fine . There's also a broader institutional support network for local producers — the Chamber of Commerce (CCISM), the agricultural cooperative SICASMART, the local-products promotion association ADEPPAL, the abattoir authority EGEA, CARIBGENE, and the beekeepers' association AAPISM — plus dedicated aid schemes: land security, startup investment support, and specific training and innovation programs for agriculture . None of this amounts to a formal tariff barrier against imported meat, but it does mean there's political will and funding behind a "buy local" push for red meat and poultry specifically — something to factor in if you're planning to compete on price in that category rather than in imported specialty cuts, seafood, or products with no meaningful local substitute.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
Saint-Martin's food safety and animal/plant health oversight runs through the French state's regional agriculture directorate (DAAF Guadeloupe), which maintains a dedicated territorial unit for the two northern islands. This unit covers Saint-Barthélemy and Saint-Martin and is responsible for food safety, animal health and protection, and agricultural economy , working alongside DAAF Guadeloupe's main food service, which handles food safety, animal health and protection, plant health and protection, and border inspection posts . Practically, this means meat, poultry, and animal products will be subject to EU-standard sanitary controls and traceability requirements — the reopened Saint-Martin abattoir itself had to secure formal sanitary approval (agrément sanitaire) before resuming operations, and its output is processed to European standards .

For plant products, phytosanitary certification is the norm across the French Antilles: exporters sending vegetal products between French Caribbean territories, to mainland France, or to third countries need to go through official certification, and it's on the exporter to check the phytosanitary conditions and requirements specific to the destination country in advance . Given Saint-Martin's small resident agricultural inspection presence, expect your import documentation (health certificates, phytosanitary certificates, veterinary certificates for meat/animal products) to be checked against standard EU/French requirements, funneled through the Guadeloupe-based DAAF infrastructure rather than a large dedicated local agency — so build a little extra lead time into your logistics for document processing and inspection scheduling.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
This market's food trade pattern is about as import-dependent as they come, and understanding why is key to spotting your opportunity. With agriculture representing less than 1% of GDP and an economy that's overwhelmingly tourism- and services-driven, virtually all food consumed on the French side — fresh produce, meat, dairy, grains, packaged goods — arrives by ship or air rather than being grown or raised locally. INSEE's price-comparison data backs this up vividly: food price levels in Saint-Martin run 47% higher than in mainland France , and dairy products, meat, pastry, cereal products, and non-alcoholic beverages contribute the most to that price gap — a strong signal that these categories make up a large and costly share of what's being imported.

On the export side, there simply isn't much of a food export sector to speak of — the domestic agricultural base (some livestock via the newly revived abattoir, small-scale produce, fishing, and beekeeping) is sized for local consumption, not export. Older trade statistics referencing sugar as a historic export good are now largely obsolete given how completely tourism has displaced agriculture as the island's economic driver.

For your business, this points clearly toward the import side of the ledger as where the real opportunity sits: a captive, non-self-sufficient market with high local prices, minimal domestic competition in most food categories, effectively duty-free entry, and a light 4% tax load. The one area to watch for competitive/political friction is red meat and poultry, where there's a deliberate local-production push (see Protected/Reserved Products above). Seafood is a nuanced case — there is local fishing, but it's not positioned as an export sector, so imported seafood (especially value-added, processed, or off-season product) likely has room to compete rather than clash with local supply.

## Other Market Context
A few things that will shape how you plan logistics, pricing, and risk on this route:

**Currency and payments:** The euro is the official currency and Saint-Martin is part of the eurozone (though it is part of the European Union but outside the Schengen Area ). In practice, given the island's deep economic integration with the Dutch side and its tourism-heavy customer base, US dollars circulate widely alongside the euro, which can simplify pricing and payment terms if you're also selling into the US market.

**Cross-island trade flows:** One underappreciated logistics point — there's no customs border between the French and Dutch sides of the island. A recent agricultural sector study notes that the island's commercial reality remains one of free movement of goods (and people) to and from Sint Maarten, alongside the coexistence of the euro and the dollar . If your business model involves warehousing or distribution decisions between the French and Dutch sides, this open internal border is a genuine operational advantage.

**Economic conditions:** The island's economy is still working through a slow recovery. Tourism, the key sector, has returned to near pre-crisis visitor levels and remains the main driver of activity , but the recovery is described as fragile, and the Dutch side retains a marked competitive edge thanks to modernized airport and port infrastructure and greater capacity . Employment is improving slowly — private salaried employment reached 7,696 positions at the end of 2024, up 0.7%, in an economy that is 85% services-based — but the unemployment rate remains high, averaging 28.8% of the active population versus 7.3% in mainland France . High unemployment alongside high food prices is a real affordability tension in this market, which is worth factoring into your product-mix and pricing strategy (value/staple lines vs. premium imports for the tourism/hospitality trade).

**Infrastructure note:** Given the Dutch side's more developed port and airport, many supply chains for the whole island — including into the French side — often route logistically through Sint Maarten's infrastructure before final distribution, something worth mapping into your freight planning regardless of where your invoicing and tax residency sit.
