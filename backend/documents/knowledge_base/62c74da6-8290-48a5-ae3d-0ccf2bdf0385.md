# Jamaica — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET) generally 10-20%, but agricultural lines run 0-40% and protected proteins carry much higher combined duty+surtax
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 15% standard GCT (effectively ~20% on commercial imports); many raw/basic foods exempt or zero-rated
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — one-way U.S. Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI) gives Jamaican exports duty-free U.S. access, but doesn't reduce Jamaica's tariffs on U.S. imports
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes — Safeguard Act protections and licensing regimes shield local harvests of produce and poultry parts
- Top imported food products: corn, wheat, soybean meal, dairy products, eggs & egg products, poultry meat (mostly from the U.S.)
- Top exported food products: bananas, coffee, cocoa/pimento, ackee, yams and root vegetables, fish/seafood

## Import Tariffs & Duties
Here's the headline: as a CARICOM member, Jamaica applies a Common External Tariff (CET) to imports from countries outside CARICOM, and the CET typically ranges between 10 and 20 percent. Goods certified as CARICOM-origin skip the CET entirely — goods imported from CARICOM countries and certified to be of CARICOM origin do not generally attract these import duties, enjoying duty-free status, though local taxes like GCT and Special Consumption Tax are still payable. So if you're ever sourcing from another CARICOM state for the Jamaican leg of your business, that's a real cost advantage.

But "10-20%" is only the average — food and agriculture is where Jamaica gets much more protective. The CARICOM CET uses ad valorem rates in bands generally from 0 to 40 percent in most cases, with common bands at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, and 40 percent, and agricultural goods sitting at the higher end. Historically, Jamaica pushed tariff reform hard in the 1990s — the main reforms included elimination of all quantitative import restrictions, tariffication of non-tariff barriers so no tariff exceeded 100 percent, and lowering of duties from 200 percent in several cases to 40 percent, phased in per the CARICOM CET. Under WTO commitments, though, Jamaica still has room to go much higher on paper: Jamaica's WTO commitments bind agricultural tariffs at 100 percent, meaning Jamaica is allowed a maximum import duty of 100 percent on products such as chicken leg quarters. This matters enormously for your protein lines specifically. Poultry and beef are the two categories where you'll feel it most: import duties and taxes on select cuts of poultry and beef in Jamaica are set at levels that significantly constrain trade, with tariffs on poultry — primarily breast meat and leg quarters — reaching as high as 250 percent, while duties on select beef cuts can climb to 80 percent. The mechanics behind that eye-watering poultry number: the CET on frozen and raw chicken leg quarters is set at 40 percent for Jamaica and most CARICOM members, but in addition to the import duty there is an Additional Stamp Duty (ASD) of 80 percent, and the import duty and ASD are compounded to arrive at a total import duty of 260 percent. Interestingly, there's a carve-out that could matter for you if you deal in lower-value poultry cuts: chicken backs and necks enjoy duty-free access to the Jamaican market due to government policies aimed at providing an inexpensive protein option for consumers. On fresh produce, expect a mixed bag — some items sail through with minimal friction, others compete directly with the local safeguard regime. Fresh fruits and vegetables that Jamaica simply can't grow well are generally welcomed: importation of particular fruits (apples, pears, strawberries, plums, kiwis) and vegetables (broccoli, asparagus, spinach) will continue to grow because very small, if any, areas in Jamaica are suitable for producing these crops. But items that compete with local farmers, like tomatoes, carrots, cabbage, melons, lettuce, and other fresh produce that compete directly with local products, are less competitive, and Jamaica's Safeguard Act of 2001 further advantages local producers, though imports of these products can increase during periods of shortages. Garlic is a bright spot for exporters: imported garlic and capsicums will remain competitive since local production is limited. On the administrative side, don't forget: importers must register with the Jamaica Customs Agency and possess a valid Tax Compliance Certificate (TCC); specific regulated goods require import permits from relevant ministries, such as Agriculture or Health, and clearance requires a Commercial Invoice, Bill of Lading or Airway Bill, and the Import Declaration (Form C87).

## Taxes
Jamaica's VAT-equivalent is the General Consumption Tax (GCT), and the standard rate matters for your landed-cost math. The standard rate of GCT is currently 15 percent, though higher or lower rates apply to certain goods and services. Critically for you as an importer, there's a wrinkle: commercial imports are taxed differently than domestic sales. With effect from April 1, 2020, the standard rate of GCT dropped from 16.5 percent to 15 percent, which also resulted in a reduction of the GCT rate on commercial imports from 21.5 percent to 20 percent. On top of that, a 5 percent advance GCT is imposed on commercial imports by registered taxpayers — this is a prepayment you can typically credit against your output GCT liability, but it does affect cash flow at the border.

The good news for a food business: a lot of what you'd actually be shipping is exempt or zero-rated. The list of items exempt from GCT includes a range of basic food items, prescription drugs, certain medical supplies, and certain construction, transportation, and financial services, while zero-rated goods and services include certain agricultural and fisheries inputs, exported goods and services, and purchases by diplomatic organizations. Specifically, on raw foodstuffs: raw foodstuff that is locally produced and raw foodstuff that is imported is exempt, including fresh fruit and vegetables — excluding imported apples, pears, quinces, apricots, cherries, peaches, nectarines, plums, sloes, berries, grapes and kiwis. Note that carve-out: several of the premium fruits you might want to bring in (grapes, berries, stone fruit) don't get the exemption, so factor GCT into your pricing on those specifically.

Beyond GCT, there are a few smaller but real line items to budget for. An environmental protection levy (EPL) and standards compliance fee (SCF) are levied at 0.5 percent and 0.3 percent respectively of the imported goods' customs value. There's also a flat administrative stamp duty: stamp duty of Jamaican $100 must be paid for shipments with a CIF value over J$5,500. And then there's the one that really bites on proteins — Additional Stamp Duty (ASD): Additional Stamp Duty is payable on certain items, e.g., chicken and most chicken parts, pork and some pork products, beef and some beef products, as well as some aluminium products, alcoholic beverages, and cigarettes. This is the mechanism behind that 260% compounded poultry rate discussed above — it's not just customs duty doing the work, it's duty plus ASD stacked together.

## Free Trade Agreements
This is the section your leadership team will want to read closely, since your model depends on moving product between the U.S. and Caribbean markets. The short version: there's no reciprocal U.S.-Jamaica free trade agreement — what exists is a one-way U.S. preference program that helps Jamaican exporters into the U.S., not the reverse. Jamaica trades with the USA under the Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI), comprising the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act (CBERA) of 1983/1990 and the 2000 Caribbean Basin Trade Partnership Act (CBTPA), which provide one-way, non-reciprocal duty-free access to the US market for goods, with a few exceptions such as footwear, luggage, flat goods, work gloves, leather apparel and watches. This program has real staying power — the CBTPA is slated to expire on September 30, 2030. For your export side (Jamaican-grown or processed product heading to your U.S. institutional buyers), this is genuinely useful: Jamaica exports most of its agricultural products to the United States, driven by demand from the large Jamaican diaspora, and these sales are facilitated by the Caribbean Basin Initiative, which grants duty-free access for most agricultural goods to the U.S. market. But there's an important recent development to flag: as of this year, that preferential treatment doesn't mean zero tariff across the board anymore. Beginning in April 2025, agricultural products from Jamaica to the United States are subject to a baseline ad valorem duty of 10 percent. So build that into your export-side cost model — CBI still beats standard MFN rates, but it's not the blanket duty-free pass it used to be.

What CBI does *not* do is lower Jamaica's tariffs on your inbound U.S.-sourced meat, grain, or produce shipments — that's governed entirely by the CARICOM CET and Jamaica's own tax structure covered above. There is a broader institutional dialogue mechanism, the CARICOM-USA Trade and Investment Framework Agreement (TIFA), signed on 28th May 2013, intended to strengthen economic relations between the USA and CARICOM countries by enhancing cooperation and promoting trade and investment, but this is a consultative framework, not a tariff-cutting instrument.

Jamaica also has agreements with other partners worth knowing about even if the USA is your priority: Jamaica has preferential tariff arrangements with the European Union under the Economic Partnership Agreement (EPA), with Canada under CARIBCAN, and with other English-speaking Caribbean states under CARICOM, and CARICOM has bilateral trade agreements with Costa Rica, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Colombia, and Venezuela — relevant if Phase II of your growth plan into Colombia, Mexico, or Central America intersects with Jamaican distribution.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans
Yes, and this is one of the more operationally important sections for a produce and protein trader. Jamaica doesn't run a simple calendar-based ban system across the board, but it does use targeted safeguards and licensing windows to protect domestic harvests and politically sensitive industries.

For fresh produce, the mechanism is the Safeguard Act: as noted above, Jamaica's Safeguard Act of 2001 advantages local producers against imports of tomatoes, carrots, cabbage, melons, lettuce and similar competing produce, though imports of these products can increase during periods of shortages. Practically, this means your ability to land these items competitively will ebb and flow with the local growing season and any weather disruptions (a live issue lately — more in Other Market Context below).

For poultry, the restriction is more of a licensing quota than a calendar ban, and it's been genuinely contentious. Chicken back and neck imports (the cheap cuts popular with lower-income households) require government-issued import permits allocated by application: Jamaica imports around US$25 million worth of chicken back and chicken neck each year, and the ministry periodically opens application windows for registered entities to apply under a "revised regime." This system has drawn real scrutiny — an investigation by the Integrity Commission recommended that the government suspend the current import procedures after finding irregularities, and importers have complained about opaque allocation: "We just get an email saying: '[Name of company redacted] is authorised to apply for two containers'... We are not aware of who gets 30 or 20 and on what basis they were approved for so much." If chicken parts are part of your product mix, budget time and relationship-building for navigating this licensing process — it's not a straightforward customs transaction.

## Protected / Reserved Products
Poultry is, by a wide margin, Jamaica's most protected food category, and it's worth understanding why before you plan around it. The Jamaican government has been offering significant protection to the poultry industry — the higher the tariff, the greater the protection local producers receive — clearly seeking to promote food security, though this protection can reduce incentives for poultry farmers to increase productivity. The scale of that protection is real: the poultry sector contributes over $1.8 billion to the Jamaican economy and is the primary beneficiary of these trade barriers. And consumption is high enough that the protected market is still worth understanding even with the tariff wall: Jamaicans are one of the highest per capita consumers of chicken in the Caribbean, though chicken is primarily produced locally due to high import duties, with only necks and backs entering duty-free. Beef faces similar, if somewhat less extreme, protection — demand for high-quality beef cuts comes primarily from the hotel industry, especially since high tariffs play a key role in limiting per capita beef consumption among Jamaicans. Given your cruise line and hotel client base, this is actually a segment worth watching closely — institutional buyers serving tourists are your best entry point precisely because they're less price-sensitive to the tariff pass-through than the general retail consumer.

Pork has its own complications, both regulatory and market-based. Key trade challenges cited by USDA include high tariffs on U.S. rice and select cuts of U.S. beef and poultry, and restricted market access for U.S. pork. On top of tariff protection, there's an outright sanitary restriction on non-canned U.S. pork (see SPS section below) — worth flagging early if pork is part of your catalog.

Sugar is another reserved-industry item, though the mechanism is licensing rather than tariff: raw and refined sugars require an import license from the Trade Board. And more broadly, JACRA (Jamaica Agricultural Commodities Regulatory Authority) plays a gatekeeping role for several commodities: JACRA is a statutory body responsible for the regulation, promotion, and standardization of the agricultural commodities industry, including cocoa, coffee, coconut and spices, and charges farmers/importers fees for dealing in specified produce.

## Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
This is a section to get right early, because permit delays are one of the most common ways new importers get stuck at the port. Different agencies own different product categories, so your compliance workflow will differ by SKU.

For meat, poultry, eggs, and animal products broadly: the Veterinary Services Division (VSD) in the Ministry of Industry, Commerce, Agriculture and Fisheries (MICAF) is responsible for regulating imports of live animals, fish and fish products, meat, poultry, and animal by-products, and red meats, poultry meats, animal products and by-products, eggs and egg products all require an import permit from VSD plus a USDA health certificate. On the U.S. export-certification side, your American suppliers will need to work through USDA's system: for meat and poultry products, an FSIS Form 9060-5 Meat and Poultry Export Certificate of Wholesomeness is required, generated through USDA's PHIS system, and importers must have a valid import permit to allow entry of meat and poultry products. Dairy has its own dual-permit structure: all dairy products imported into Jamaica must be accompanied by an import permit from the Director of Veterinary Services and an export certificate from the competent authority, and powdered milk and milk-based products require both an import permit from Veterinary Services and an import license from the Trade Board. For fresh produce and plant products, the relevant authority shifts to Plant Quarantine: all plants and plant parts exported to Jamaica must be accompanied by an import permit issued by the Plant Quarantine Division of MICAF and a phytosanitary certificate issued by the competent authority of the exporting country. Rice and cornmeal fall under this too: an importer of fresh produce, rice and cornmeal must obtain a Phytosanitary certificate from MICAF. The application process now runs through a digital portal: to register, you'll need a Jamaican Taxpayer Registration Number (TRN) and valid identification, after which you log into your JSWIFT account and apply for a Phytosanitary Import Permit, selecting "Plant Import Permit" as the process type. A few outright prohibitions worth knowing before you build a catalog: the importation of honey and by-products is strictly prohibited, live or processed conch and lobster is strictly prohibited (for personal imports; commercial seafood trade runs through separate fisheries permitting), and notably for U.S. suppliers, the importation of pork and pork products, excluding hermetically sealed cans/tins, from the USA is strictly prohibited. That last one is a real constraint if fresh or frozen U.S. pork is part of your planned catalog — canned/shelf-stable pork is your workaround, fresh/frozen isn't currently an option from U.S. origin.

One more practical note: these rules move. Requirements may be adjusted at any time as disease situations change, so build a habit of confirming current permit conditions before each new shipment rather than assuming last quarter's rules still apply — animal disease outbreaks (African Swine Fever, avian influenza, etc.) can trigger sudden country- or region-specific restrictions.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products
Jamaica's trade pattern tells a clear story, and it maps well onto your business model. On the import side, this is a country that leans hard on the U.S. for grain, protein inputs, and processed consumer foods: U.S. agricultural exports to Jamaica in 2024 totaled approximately $590.59 million, ranking Jamaica the 36th largest U.S. agricultural export market, with a three-year average of $609.55 million. The top individual commodities are telling — corn led at $66.54 million, followed by ethanol at $58.93 million, soybean meal at $55.82 million, eggs and products at $48.27 million, wheat at $48.13 million, baked goods at $38.69 million, dairy products at $33.45 million, soybean oil at $25.45 million, food preparations at $23.85 million, and poultry meat and products at $22.27 million. Overall food import volume is substantial: the total value of food and beverage imports to Jamaica in 2024 totaled $1.3 billion, with approximately 40 percent supplied by the United States. And critically for your institutional-buyer strategy, most of it doesn't go to retail: approximately 60 percent of food imports are destined for the hotel, restaurant, and institutional (HRI) sector, which lines up almost exactly with your cruise line, hotel, and food-distributor customer base.

On the export side, Jamaica splits its output into two buckets. Traditional exports are the legacy commodity crops: bananas, citrus, coffee, cocoa and pimento; bauxite, alumina and gypsum; as well as sugar, rum and products made from citrus, coffee and cocoa. Non-traditional exports are where the more diversified, higher-value opportunity sits: cucumbers, pumpkins, dasheens, sweet potatoes, yams, mangoes, papayas, ackee, and fish, alongside cut flowers and other light manufactured goods. Blue Mountain coffee and other premium specialty items remain flagship export categories too: Jamaica's agricultural exports are centered on Blue Mountain coffee, sugar and rum, bananas, mangoes, yams, ackee, citrus, and specialty cocoa. What this means for you concretely: the opportunity on the import side is squarely in the categories Jamaica structurally can't produce at scale — wheat, corn, soybean meal, and premium/off-season fruit and vegetables — sold into the HRI channel your business is already built around. The competitive pressure is concentrated in poultry, beef, and to a lesser extent pork and staple vegetables, where tariffs, ASD, and licensing exist specifically to blunt foreign competition — those are categories to price and plan around carefully rather than lead with. On the export side, Jamaica's non-traditional produce (yams, ackee, callaloo, root vegetables, tropical fruit) and seafood are exactly the kind of premium, diaspora- and tourism-driven goods your "direct global agricultural supply partner" model is designed to move north into the U.S. under CBI preference — this is arguably your more natural growth lane out of Jamaica, rather than trying to import U.S. protein into a heavily protected local market.

## Other Market Context
A few things beyond tariffs and permits that will shape how this market actually feels to operate in day to day.

Currency and macro conditions have been volatile lately due to Hurricane Melissa, which made landfall on October 28, 2025, and this is the single biggest near-term factor affecting Jamaica's food economy. Jamaica's agricultural sector sustained substantial damage due to Hurricane Melissa. The reconstruction damage estimate is staggering: damage to critical infrastructure stands at approximately US$8.8 billion — more than 40 percent of Jamaica's GDP. That's translating directly into food-price and import pressure: Jamaica's annual inflation rate reached 7.5 percent in July 2026, rising from 6.7 percent in June and 3.3 percent a year earlier, and a rise in agricultural inflation amid worsening drought and heat conditions is expected to influence higher prices over the next few months. The central bank does see this as temporary rather than structural: inflation is projected to return to the 4.0 to 6.0 percent target range by the end of the December 2026 quarter, and reserves remain a stabilizing factor — the country retains healthy international reserves, while a relatively stable exchange rate provides a useful buffer against imported inflation. For your business, the practical read is: near-term local supply gaps (especially in produce categories hit by the storm) may actually create short-term import opportunities even in normally-protected categories, since Jamaica has historically allowed produce imports to rise during shortage periods.

Growth outlook is modest but positive: GDP growth is expected to remain within the projected range of 1.0 to 3.0 percent for fiscal year 2026/27, with recovery spending actually supporting demand — selected indicators point to improving demand conditions in Jamaica in the context of a gradual post-Hurricane Melissa recovery. On interest rates, the central bank has been easing: the Monetary Policy Committee decided to reduce the policy rate by 25 basis points to 5.50 percent per annum as of February 2026, reflecting improving conditions at that time (rates and outlook have shifted since with the inflation uptick, so confirm the current policy rate before finalizing any local financing plans).

On logistics: given the storm damage to roads and infrastructure, expect some continued disruption to inland distribution and cold-chain reliability in the near term, which reinforces the case for the staged logistics approach in your plan — leaning on established freight partners now, with proprietary capability (Majestic Air Cargo) phased in as the market stabilizes and infrastructure rebuilds. Given the concentration of your target customers (cruise lines, hotels, resorts) in tourism-dependent demand, it's also worth watching the Bank of Jamaica's repeated flagging of tourism-sector risk from global shocks, since a slowdown there would directly affect the HRI-channel demand your Jamaica strategy depends on.
