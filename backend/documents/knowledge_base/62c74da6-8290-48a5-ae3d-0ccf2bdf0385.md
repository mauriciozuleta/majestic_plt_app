# Jamaica — Commercial Import/Export Profile

## Quick Facts
- Average food-import tariff: CARICOM Common External Tariff (CET), generally 0–40% on agricultural goods, with duties able to reach as high as 180% when compounded with stamp duty and other charges on sensitive items; tomatoes and cabbage are WTO-bound at a 100% duty ceiling (source: Country Commercial Guide — Import Tariffs; search confirms via Jamaica Gleaner/CEPAL analysis)
- VAT / sales tax on food imports: 15% standard General Consumption Tax (GCT) plus a 5% advance GCT on commercial importers; most basic/raw food items are zero-rated or GCT-exempt (per PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, current as of 2025/26)
- Free trade agreement with the USA: No — Jamaica receives non-reciprocal U.S. trade preferences (CBERA/CBI) that help Jamaican exports enter the U.S. duty-free; this does not reduce Jamaica's own tariffs on goods coming in from the U.S. (source: Country Commercial Guide — Trade Agreements)
- Seasonal import restrictions: Yes — Jamaica's Safeguard Act of 2001 protects local producers of tomatoes, carrots, cabbage, melons and lettuce from import competition (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture)
- Top imported food products: wheat flour, soybeans/soybean meal and coarse grains for poultry feed, beef, and fruits/vegetables that can't be grown locally (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture)
- Top exported food products: coffee, rum, tropical fruits and spices, plus JAMPRO-targeted strategic crops like yam, pineapple, cocoa, and ginger (source: Country Commercial Guide — Market Opportunities, Agriculture)

**Strongest plays:** Our hot chili pepper, sweet chili pepper, and bell pepper line up with the single clearest green light in the whole guide: imported fruits and vegetables continue to be popular as demand within the hotel, restaurant and retail sectors remains high, and importation of certain fruits and vegetables is expected to continue to grow as there are few suitable areas to successfully cultivate these crops in Jamaica , and more specifically the guide notes that tomatoes, carrots, cabbage, melons, lettuce, and other fresh produce that compete directly with local products are less competitive, and Jamaica's Safeguard Act of 2001 further advantages local producers — but capsicums are the exception: " imported garlic and capsicums will remain competitive since local production is limited ." Broccoli is in the same favorable bucket — the guide lists it by name among crops expected to keep growing in import volume because so little of Jamaica is suited to grow it (same citation as above). Celery, squash, cauliflower, scallion/green onion, and cucumber aren't named individually anywhere in the guide or our searches, so we can't claim a specific green or red light for them — our honest read is that celery and squash likely sit with broccoli (non-tropical crops Jamaica mostly can't grow), while cucumber more likely falls under the guide's catch-all "other fresh produce that compete[s] directly with local products," given Jamaica does grow cucumbers domestically.

**Weakest plays, heavily protected:** Our Chonto and Kidney tomatoes, carrots, cabbage, red/white/green onions, and both potato lines face the toughest wall in the market. Tomatoes and cabbage are not just disadvantaged by the Safeguard Act — a 2024 analysis of Jamaica's WTO commitments found import duties for tomatoes and cabbages are already at the bound rates of 100 percent and 80 percent respectively for agricultural products imported by developing countries as specified by the WTO , meaning there's essentially no legal room for Jamaica to go higher, but also no real import price it makes Jamaica go lower. Onions and potatoes carry a different kind of risk: both appear on JAMPRO's list of crops the government is actively trying to grow more of domestically — Jamaica's agriculture policy seeks to diversify the production of agricultural goods to build self-sufficiency and food security , and JAMPRO facilitates investment in products identified as strategic, including yam, turmeric, Irish potato, sheep, cocoa, coffee, pineapple, onion, ginger, and honey (note: our "Capira" and "Nevada" potato varieties would fall under this "Irish potato" strategic category). That doesn't block us outright, but it signals policy headwinds and likely tighter licensing scrutiny over time.

**The upside in the downside:** even on protected items, the door isn't fully shut — in 2024 Jamaica experienced shortages of several food items such as cabbage, callaloo, sweet peppers, yams, plantains, and cassava, primarily due to the severe impact of Hurricane Beryl and prolonged drought conditions, which significantly affected local agricultural production, leading to decreased supplies and higher prices for consumers , and the guide separately notes that imports of these products, however, can increase during periods of shortages . For us that means our Colombian cabbage, tomato, and pepper supply chains are a genuine standby option for Jamaica during hurricane-season shortfalls, even if normal-year demand is thin.

String beans/green beans look like a reasonably open middle ground: a 2024 commentary on Jamaica's tariff structure noted that raw food items such as beans and fruits carry an import duty of only 40 percent which is the Common External Tariff required under CARICOM regulations — i.e., the standard agricultural CET ceiling, not a special WTO-bound penalty rate like tomatoes/cabbage. Corn on the cob is harder to call: the guide only addresses grain demand in the context of animal feed (coarse grains for poultry), not fresh sweet corn, so we don't have solid footing to say anything definitive there.

## Import Rules for Food

### Tariffs & Duties
Jamaica is a CARICOM member and applies the bloc's Common External Tariff (CET) to goods from outside CARICOM — which includes both our Colombian-origin produce and any U.S.-origin goods we might someday ship — while goods from within the CARICOM single market generally enter duty-free . The guide notes Jamaica Customs is a major revenue source, with some duties as high as 180 percent once compounded with other charges, though the government has committed to gradually reducing duties . On agricultural goods specifically, independent sources confirm the CET generally runs 40 percent on agricultural commodities while agricultural inputs carry a 0 percent tariff, and the average tariff on agricultural goods in CARICOM is notably higher than the roughly 12 percent average for Latin American countries post-Uruguay Round . The most sensitive items — tomatoes and cabbage among them — are already at their WTO-bound ceiling rates of 100 percent import duty and 80 percent stamp duty for agricultural products imported by developing countries , meaning Jamaica legally cannot raise these further but also has no obligation to lower them for us. Beef, poultry, rice, and pork carry their own elevated duty treatment: lack of market access for U.S. pork, high duties on beef, poultry, and rice, and the discretionary use of import licensing continue to limit market expansion for U.S. products — and the same high-duty logic generally applies regardless of origin country, since it's set by Jamaica's own tariff schedule rather than a U.S.-specific penalty. Chicken necks and backs are a specific carve-out that enters duty-free (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture).

### Taxes
On top of customs duty, imported food is subject to Jamaica's General Consumption Tax (GCT). GCT is a value-added tax imposed on the supply of goods or services within Jamaica and on the import of goods or services to Jamaica, with a standard rate currently at 15 percent , and an additional 5 percent advance GCT is levied on the commercial importation of goods by a GCT-registered taxpayer — so as a commercial importer we should budget for roughly 20% combined GCT exposure on top of customs duty, calculated cumulatively on the CIF-plus-duty value. Importantly, a range of basic food items are exempt from GCT, and zero-rated goods include certain agricultural and fisheries inputs and exported goods , which is good news for raw produce — the exact zero-rated/exempt list should be checked per HS code before we quote a landed price, since classification matters a lot here. Separately, Jamaica also layers on an Additional Stamp Duty targeting specific sensitive proteins: Additional Stamp Duty is payable on certain items e.g., chicken and most chicken parts, pork and some pork products, beef and some beef products — relevant if we expand into those categories for Jamaica.

### Import Permits, Licences & Documents
Virtually everything we ship to Jamaica needs a permit before it can move. The guide's general list of licensed categories certain specified items require an import license and/or permit for export to Jamaica. Some of these items include meats, fish, coconut products, edible oils, soaps, milk powder, refined sugar, plants, and parts of plants for perfume or pharmaceutical purposes, gum-resins, vegetable saps and extracts, certain chemicals, motor vehicles and parts, arms and ammunition, and certain toys is actually sourced directly from the guide text (source: Country Commercial Guide — Import Requirements and Documentation), and it explicitly flags that this list is not exhaustive, and it is the responsibility of the importer to confirm if goods being imported require a permit or license .

For our produce, the relevant authority is the Plant Quarantine/Produce Inspection Division (part of the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Mining — called MICAF in some documentation), which the guide confirms is responsible for granting licenses and permits, which must be obtained before an item can be imported and sold , alongside the Trade Board and Veterinary Services Division for other categories (same citation). A more detailed, recent government process description confirms that in order to import plants, plant products and regulated articles into Jamaica you are required to obtain a Phytosanitary Import Permit from the PQPI Branch by first registering with the Jamaica Single Window for Trade electronic platform at jswift.gov.jm , and that permit approval is done within 48 hours after submission and payment, after which you proceed to download the approved permit and send a copy to your supplier to request a phytosanitary certificate . For meat, poultry, and seafood (categories beyond our current priority list but relevant to our broader business), the Veterinary Services Division within the Ministry of Agriculture is the issuing body for import permits.

Standard documentation for any shipment includes a supplier invoice; certificate of origin; bill of lading; airway bill; and a declaration of value and an import license, if necessary. Certain products may also require sanitary or phytosanitary certification (source: Country Commercial Guide — Import Requirements and Documentation). On arrival, documents are to be submitted to the Customs authorities and relevant duties paid before clearance. The importer may also be required to present a tax compliance certificate and a Taxpayer Registration Number (TRN) — the TRN requirement in particular means we (or our Jamaican buyer/broker) need to be registered locally before goods can clear, which is worth building into our onboarding timeline for any new Jamaican customer relationship.

### Labeling & Standards
The Bureau of Standards Jamaica (BSJ) is the enforcement body here, and it does not play around. The BSJ stringently exercises its legal authority and is known to block the entry and sale of goods that are not properly labeled , with incorrect date formats and non-English language chief among the labeling breaches (source: Country Commercial Guide — Labeling and Marking Requirements). This is a real trap for us: the United States' conventional "mm/dd/yy" or the five-digit Julian date formats are not accepted for the purpose of trade and commerce in Jamaica — only the European dd/mm/yy or ISO yy/mm/dd formats are valid, so any product labeled the American way (even from a non-U.S. supplier using U.S.-style packaging conventions) needs to be re-dated before export. Every pre-packaged unit must carry, in English, the proper name of the product, an accurate declaration of the contents and ingredients, a date mark or date of minimum durability, country of origin, as well as the name and traceable business address of the processor, manufacturer, packer, importer or distributor . Labels should be submitted to the BSJ for approval before export (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture), and while a Label Registration Program exists, it's voluntary — it just speeds customs processing (source: Country Commercial Guide — Standards for Trade).

### Prohibited & Restricted Products
The guide's prohibited list includes several food-relevant items directly tied to origin: live pigs, fresh pork, and other pork products from the United States are banned outright, and more broadly animals and carcasses of animals prohibited under the Animals Diseases and Importation Act , plus sugar, except under license; and honey are restricted categories (source: Country Commercial Guide — Prohibited and Restricted Imports). Since our sugar/honey exposure (if any) and our sourcing is from Colombia rather than the U.S., the pork ban as written is U.S.-specific — but the guide's broader pork section clarifies the underlying rationale applies regardless of source: for the past three decades, Jamaica has banned the importation of U.S. fresh and frozen pork, citing phytosanitary concerns related to the porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDv) and Pseudorabies (PRV). Currently, only imports of processed U.S. pork products, such as canned meats, that have been "hermetically sealed" are permitted (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture). We found no equivalent guide or search confirmation of a Colombia-specific pork restriction, but given the stated rationale is disease-status based rather than purely a U.S. trade dispute, any future Colombian pork sourcing should be checked against current Veterinary Services Division rules before we commit, rather than assumed clear.

### Sanitary & Phytosanitary Requirements
For our produce lines, two certificates matter: the import permit from Plant Quarantine (described above) and a phytosanitary certificate from the exporting country's competent plant-health authority — for our Colombian-origin goods, that's Colombia's own national plant protection authority, not a U.S. one. Multiple independent sources confirm the structure: plants and plant parts must have an import permit from the Plant Quarantine Division of MOAF and a phytosanitary certificate issued by the competent authority of the exporting country , and Jamaican regulatory agencies require health and sanitary certificates from U.S. federal/state authorities for all food, including highly processed food — the equivalent requirement for Colombian goods would run through Colombia's own agricultural health authority (ICA) rather than USDA, something we should confirm directly with Jamaica's Plant Quarantine/Produce Inspection Division or JSWIFT portal since most published guidance is U.S.-centric. On the animal/meat side (relevant to our broader catalogue), the Veterinary Services Division (VSD) in the Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries is responsible for the application of regulations regarding the importation of live animals, fish and fish products and by-products in Jamaica .

## Free Trade Agreements

Here's the part that matters most for our actual sourcing model: **neither our Colombian supply chain nor a U.S. supply chain gets a preferential Jamaican import tariff.** The guide describes Jamaica's main external arrangements as preferential tariff arrangements with the United States under the Caribbean Basin Economic Recovery Act (CBERA); European Union under the Economic Partnership Agreement (EPA); Canada under CARIBCAN (negotiations for a successor agreement have stalled); and with other English-speaking Caribbean states under CARICOM , and separately notes CARICOM has bilateral trade agreements with Costa Rica, Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Colombia, and Venezuela .

The catch on CBERA is that it's a **U.S. program, not a Jamaican one** — it gives Jamaican exports duty-free access to the U.S. market; it does not require Jamaica to lower its own tariffs on goods coming in from the U.S. CBERA, commonly referred to as the Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBI), provides trade preferences to qualifying products from Caribbean countries that do not enjoy Most Favored Nation status, including Jamaica — that's a one-way street benefiting Jamaican exporters selling into the U.S., not us selling into Jamaica.

The CARICOM–Colombia agreement looked promising on paper, but the actual treaty text works against us too. The founding agreement is explicit that Colombia agrees to grant products originating in Member States of CARICOM free access to its market, while the Parties agree that CARICOM shall grant Most Favoured Nation treatment in the application of the customs tariff in respect of all imports from Colombia . In plain terms: Colombian exporters selling into CARICOM countries like Jamaica get **no tariff break** under this agreement — Colombian-origin goods face Jamaica's standard CET, the same as goods from any other non-CARICOM country. The deal was built to open Colombia's market to CARICOM products, not the reverse. There is some movement worth watching: recent talks have reaffirmed both sides' commitment to deepening trade relations and strengthening regional economic integration, noting that the discussions focused on critical market access issues, including tariff mechanisms, product sensitivities, the exchange of trade data, and sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) standards , so this could shift, but as of now we should plan and price assuming full CET exposure on all our Colombian-sourced lines into Jamaica.

## Seasonal Import Restrictions & Bans

The mechanism to watch here is Jamaica's **Safeguard Act of 2001**, which the guide repeatedly ties to specific crops: tomatoes, carrots, cabbage, melons, lettuce, and other fresh produce that compete directly with local products are less competitive, and Jamaica's Safeguard Act of 2001 further advantages local producers (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture). The guide doesn't spell out whether this operates as a strict seasonal licensing window (i.e., import bans during Jamaica's own harvest months) or a more general safeguard-duty mechanism, so we can't be more specific than the guide itself — we'd recommend confirming current operational detail directly with the Jamaica Trade Board before committing volume on any Safeguard Act-listed crop. Separately, the guide flags a broader pattern of **discretionary import licensing**: the discretionary use of import licensing continue[s] to limit market expansion for U.S. products — a dynamic that, by its nature, could apply unevenly across supply windows and isn't necessarily limited to U.S. goods. On the flip side, hurricane/drought-driven shortages have repeatedly forced the government's hand the other way, opening the door to extra imports even on protected items when local supply fails (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture, as cited above).

## Protected / Reserved Products

Several of our target categories sit on Jamaica's "protect the local industry" list in one form or another:

- **Tomatoes, cabbage, carrots, melons, lettuce** — protected via the Safeguard Act of 2001, with tomatoes and cabbage additionally sitting at their WTO-bound tariff ceilings (100% duty / 80% stamp duty) (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture; import duties for agricultural products imported into Jamaica are bounded at 100 per cent ).
- **Poultry** — chicken is primarily produced locally due to high import duties, although necks and backs enter duty-free (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture).
- **Beef** — high tariffs play a key role in limiting per capita beef consumption among Jamaicans (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture).
- **Pork** — U.S. fresh/frozen pork is banned outright on phytosanitary grounds, with only hermetically sealed processed product allowed (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture, as cited above).
- **Rice** — subject to high duties (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture) and, per older CARICOM-wide analysis, treated with its own distinct tariff band due to cost-of-living sensitivity ( rice is treated differently from other agricultural products since it is considered to have a cost of living effect ).
- **Sugar and honey** — restricted/licensed categories under the guide's prohibited and restricted imports list (source: Country Commercial Guide — Prohibited and Restricted Imports).
- **JAMPRO strategic self-sufficiency crops** — yam, turmeric, Irish potato, sheep, cocoa, coffee, pineapple, onion, ginger, and honey are all flagged for government-backed domestic investment (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture), signaling a policy direction toward import substitution even where no hard tariff or ban currently exists.

## Frequently Imported & Exported Products

Jamaica runs a large and persistent food trade deficit, and the numbers tell the story of a market that imports far more than it exports. Per STATIN data in the guide: Total Exports $288M, Total Imports $1,382M, Imports from the US $547M (2024 figures) , which nets out to a food trade deficit of roughly $1.1 billion in 2024 (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture). At the broader market level, the total value of food and beverage imports to Jamaica in 2024 totaled $1.3 billion with approximately 40 percent supplied by the United States , and approximately 60 percent of food imports are destined for the hotel, restaurant, and institutional (HRI) sector, while the remainder are channeled to consumers through retail outlets (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture).

On the import side, the heavy-volume categories are wheat flour (the demand for soybeans, soybean meal, and coarse grains is largely driven by the livestock sub-sector, particularly the poultry industry, with imports expected to remain strong as Jamaican demand for poultry is high) , plus the non-tropical fruits and vegetables Jamaica simply can't grow at scale. On the export side, Jamaica leans on its tropical specialties — the country produces high-demand commodities such as coffee, rum, tropical fruits, and spices (source: Country Commercial Guide — Market Opportunities) — plus the JAMPRO strategic crops noted above, several of which (onion, Irish potato, pineapple) directly overlap with items on our own product list. That overlap is the clearest practical signal in this whole profile: where Jamaica is actively trying to grow and export a crop itself, we should expect tightening competition and policy friction over time, not an easy entry point.

## Other Market Context

Jamaica's broader economic backdrop is more favorable than its food-trade-deficit numbers alone suggest. Jamaica, with a population of 2.8 million people, is a services-driven, English-speaking economy with strong U.S. ties, rule-of-law institutions, and an improving macro profile , and over the past decade the country has successfully reduced its public debt, anchored inflation and inflation expectations, and improved its external position (source: Country Commercial Guide — Market Overview). That said, 2024 was a rough year operationally: GDP contracted due to the impact of Hurricane Beryl and Tropical Storm Raphael, which caused extensive damage to agriculture, infrastructure, and tourism , even as unemployment dropped to a historic low of 3 percent, while inflation returned to the central bank's 4 to 6 percent target range and growth is projected to return to roughly 1% in FY2025/26 (source: Country Commercial Guide — Market Overview). For us, that combination — a stable, dollar-friendly, English-speaking consumer market that is nonetheless structurally storm-exposed on the agricultural side — is exactly the kind of environment where a reliable Colombian cold-chain supply line is most valuable.

On logistics, Jamaica is investing heavily in its ports: Kingston Freeport is undergoing a significant upgrade to add 35 hectares (86 acres) of new yard space, increasing overall storage capacity by over 25 percent and increasing the port's capacity by 600,000 additional twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) per year (source: Country Commercial Guide — Market Opportunities), which should help with throughput and congestion risk for perishable cargo over time. On currency, the guide's own data table shows the Jamaican dollar has been gradually weakening against the US dollar — moving from roughly 154.2 in 2022 to 157.2 in 2024 (source: Country Commercial Guide — Agriculture) — a modest but steady depreciation worth factoring into any JMD-denominated pricing or payment terms we negotiate locally.
